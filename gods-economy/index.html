Faith · Economics · Stewardship

God’s Economy

Money, mercy, and the limits of every earthly system

Michael Emanuel Glover · October 26, 2026

01 The coin and the kingdom

A coin can buy bread. It cannot tell us whether we ought to share it.

That distinction belongs at the beginning of a Christian account of economics. Money is a remarkable human tool: a means of exchange, a unit of account, and a way of carrying purchasing power into an uncertain future. It can help a stranger feed another stranger without either knowing the other's name. It can finance a workshop, pay a nurse, repair a roof, and preserve resources against the winter.

The same instrument can organize exploitation, reward deception, finance murder, and persuade its owner that possession is a substitute for goodness. Its usefulness does not make it holy. Its dangers do not make every use of it sinful.

Christ's warning about serving God and mammon concerns allegiance. Wealth becomes an idol when its accumulation acquires authority over the conscience: when a person will sacrifice truth, neighbor, family, or creation to keep the account growing. Treasure trains the heart. The Christian question is therefore larger than whether a transaction makes money. It asks what kind of person, household, and society the transaction helps us become. 1

God's economy, as the phrase is used here, means the ordering of our material life under God's moral will. It is neither a claim that Scripture contains a modern macroeconomic textbook nor a promise that the faithful will become rich. It begins with creation as gift, human beings as bearers of God's image, and Jesus Christ as Lord. The crucified and risen Christ judges our success by a measure that a balance sheet cannot supply.

Money belongs in the household. It does not belong on the throne.

02 Take economics seriously

An explicitly Christian essay should be honest about scarcity. Time spent on one task cannot simultaneously be spent on every other task. Resources have alternative uses. Incentives affect behavior. Specialization, exchange, investment, and innovation can increase what people are able to produce and enjoy. Prices often coordinate dispersed decisions more effectively than a distant authority could manage in detail. 2, 3

The position here is deliberately compatible with the mainstream neoclassical tradition as extended by modern work on information, institutions, imperfect competition, and behavior. That tradition provides analytical tools, not a single party platform. It contains serious disagreements about policy, measurement, and distribution. No honest invocation of an economic consensus should erase those disagreements.

Under demanding conditions, competitive markets have powerful efficiency properties. Those conditions matter. Market power, external costs, missing insurance, public goods, and unequal information can change the result. Efficiency also does not settle distribution: an allocation can be efficient in the technical sense while leaving someone desperately poor. Modern economics gives us reasons to study both market failure and the costs, incentives, and informational limits of proposed remedies. 2, 4

A Christian may therefore defend property, enterprise, competition, and voluntary exchange while supporting effective public institutions, social insurance, and environmental protection. A Christian may also question a particular intervention when it wastes resources, shelters insiders, or harms the people it promises to help. Neither a market mechanism nor a government program receives moral immunity from its name.

Good intentions do not remove opportunity costs. Neither do opportunity costs remove the obligation to care.

03 What marx saw, and what we need not accept

Many forms of capitalism are plainly money-making regimes. Capital is advanced in expectation of a financial return; firms face pressure to remain solvent, attract investment, and earn profits. Describing this is not an insult. It identifies an organizing incentive.

Marx gave that incentive a memorable expression in Capital: M–C–M′, money committed to commodities and production with the aim of returning as more money. That description catches something important about accumulation. It also helps explain why an organization can pursue expansion after its owners already have enough for a comfortable life. The process can become an end that continually renews itself. 5

A Christian can respect this observation without accepting Marx's account of value, his full theory of exploitation, his materialist philosophy, or an inevitable revolutionary destiny. The labor theory of value is not the organizing foundation of modern mainstream price theory. Returns to investment may compensate deferred consumption, risk, coordination, innovation, and productive capital. A profitable enterprise can genuinely serve its neighbors. A loss-making enterprise can still behave unjustly.

Yet legal agreement alone does not establish equal bargaining power. Workers may face limited alternatives, costly moves, incomplete information, and urgent needs. Owners may possess leverage that a formal right to decline a contract does little to offset. This is where part of Marx's critique deserves an empirical hearing, rather than ceremonial dismissal.

Consider a specific finding. A 2018 IMF working paper studied publicly traded firms in 74 economies during 1980–2016. It estimated that average markups in advanced economies had risen by 39 percent since 1980, with the increase concentrated among high-markup firms. It also found a generally negative association between markups and firms' labor shares. The sample is not every business, markups are estimated rather than directly observed, and association alone does not establish causation. Still, the result gives concrete substance to concern about market power. 6

Or consider labor-market evidence. Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, and Zipperer examined 138 prominent US state minimum-wage changes between 1979 and 2016. Their study found higher low-end earnings with the overall number of low-wage jobs essentially unchanged over the following five years. This is evidence against assuming that every observed wage is already the best attainable outcome for workers. It neither proves Marx's system nor establishes that any minimum wage, at any level, is harmless; it also does not by itself identify employer power as the explanation. 7

These findings support a limited conclusion: competition and bargaining conditions deserve scrutiny. They do not validate every Marxian mechanism, forecast, or remedy. Respect for Marx is strongest when it is discriminating enough to disagree with him.

04 Profit is a signal; love is a command

Profit can signal that customers value a product more than the priced resources used to provide it. It can also reflect monopoly power, privileged access, or costs imposed on people outside the transaction. Its moral meaning depends on how it was earned and what the accounting leaves out.

A factory may pay every invoice while polluting a river. A financial intermediary may record revenue while selling confusion. Conversely, a business may earn an honest surplus by reducing waste, making a useful medicine, or building a dependable service. Moral judgment requires attention to the activity, the alternatives, and the people affected.

Christian love does not require pretending that incentives disappear after conversion. It requires refusing to treat a neighbor as expendable whenever exploitation happens to pay. James's condemnation of withheld wages places economic conduct within judgment before God. The employer's obligation is not exhausted by finding a clever defense of the payroll. 8

Nor can a humane economy be built entirely from occasional acts of personal generosity. Rules structure the choices available to millions of people who will never meet. Competition policy, enforceable contracts, honest accounts, worker protection, and well-designed assistance can express public concern for those neighbors. Each proposal must still be examined for effectiveness and unintended consequences.

Production matters enormously. The World Bank's 2024 poverty report called for more inclusive growth while documenting how weak growth, inequality, and overlapping crises obstruct poverty reduction. That is reason to care about both the size of the material provision and people's access to it. It is not evidence that one pure institutional formula deserves all credit for development. 9

A Christian economy should want more bread where people are hungry. It should also ask who can reach the table.

05 God's universe does not accept our excuses

Economic life takes place within creation. In Christian terms, God's natural laws are not obstacles erected outside His providence. They describe the regularities of a world we have been given to inhabit and tend. Recognizing them is one form of intellectual obedience.

Agriculture depends on living soils, water, temperature, plant biology, and time. Workers need nourishment, rest, and care. Bodies cannot be made infinitely productive by increasing the urgency of a manager's demands. Cargo does not cross an ocean without material arrangements. A spreadsheet cannot repeal a drought.

Weather and climate are different, but both matter economically. A storm can interrupt a harvest or a supply route; a changing climate can alter the risks facing whole regions. The IPCC's assessments identify observed damage to food and water security and growing risks across connected systems. Prudent stewardship includes taking those constraints seriously, rather than treating environmental costs as someone else's problem. 10

Trade can reduce waste and move goods toward people who value them more. It does not create unlimited hours, frictionless transport, or infinite human attention. Some transaction costs can fall dramatically; others remain stubborn. There is no established universal numerical ceiling for every measure of future economic efficiency. There are physical limits to particular processes, and persistent problems of information, coordination, and care.

We should welcome better technology without turning technological possibility into a promise of omnipotence. A machine may improve a harvest. It cannot make the distinction between stewardship and plunder morally irrelevant.

06 Entropy is real; economic destiny is not a physics equation

The second law of thermodynamics constrains physical production. Real transformations involve irreversibility; useful work cannot be obtained from every energy source without limits and losses. Maintenance, repair, and the provision of usable energy remain material concerns, however sophisticated our financial arrangements become. 11

But an economy is not an isolated thermodynamic system. Earth receives energy from the Sun and emits energy to space. Local organization and long periods of flourishing are compatible with the second law. Entropy does not, by itself, predict next year's recession, establish a falling rate of profit, or prove that a particular political system must collapse. There is no meaningful scientific procedure in which we substitute GDP into an entropy formula and read off the date of judgment. 11, 12

Here, then, is a supposition to consider: no human economy can secure ultimate and eternal stability by its own arrangements. This is a Christian philosophical judgment informed by physical finitude and human fallibility, not a demonstrated theorem of economics. It must not be smuggled into the argument as something the reader has already agreed to prove.

By stability I mean more than the ability to recover from a recession or preserve institutions for generations. I mean a permanent guarantee against destructive disruption, moral corruption, and the need for renewal. Rejecting that ultimate guarantee leaves considerable room for practical stability, successful reform, and durable prosperity. Indeed, it makes their cultivation more urgent.

We maintain a bridge because it can fail. We should maintain institutions for the same reason.

07 Sin in the model, sin in the mirror

Human economic systems are adaptive and interdependent. Expectations change behavior; behavior changes the world that forecasters are trying to describe. Some relationships are stable enough to estimate usefully, while others shift under new institutions or shocks. Some systems exhibit nonlinear and chaotic behavior. None of that licenses the claim that every economic process is mathematically chaotic or that careful forecasting is worthless.

Forecasting becomes arrogant when its practitioner confuses a conditional model with exhaustive knowledge. Hayek's warning about the pretence of knowledge remains useful here: important information is dispersed, and numerical precision can exceed what our understanding warrants. Humility should improve our methods, not abolish them. We can forecast, state uncertainty, compare outcomes with predictions, and revise. 13

Greed is not the only sin that destabilizes an economy. Malice can destroy a livelihood for pleasure. Murder and war can convert productive capacity into organized ruin. The lust for power can corrupt a regulator, a company, a union, a ministry, or a revolution. The hunger for recognition can lead an expert to conceal a failed prediction or a philanthropist to value applause above the people supposedly served.

Private ownership does not cure these temptations. Public ownership does not cure them either. Christians should expect institutions to need accountability because the people within them remain capable of self-deception. That includes the Christian writing about everyone else's sins.

Checks on power, transparent evidence, honest disagreement, and opportunities to correct mistakes are practical expressions of this humility. Repentance also has an economic dimension: ending the fraud, paying what is owed, restoring what was taken, and accepting limits on one's own discretion.

08 What outlasts the coin

James pictures accumulated gold and silver as corroded, their decay becoming testimony against their possessors. The image strips wealth of its claim to permanence. A chemical qualification matters: pure gold resists ordinary corrosion, while silver tarnishes and the other metals in gold alloys can corrode. Coins can nevertheless wear, melt, be dispersed, or lose the form and authority that made them money. The moral warning does not require us to assert that pure gold rusts like iron. 8, 14

No coin is a covenant of eternal security. Even an intact coin cannot promise that its owner will live to spend it, that another person will accept it, or that the purchase will be good.

God's moral will has a different standing. In the Christian confession offered here, truth, justice, mercy, fidelity, and love do not expire when a currency fails or a theory goes out of fashion. Our understanding of their application can be mistaken and needs correction. Their authority does not derive from the exchange rate.

This is why the Christian hope cannot finally rest in an indefinitely rising index, a perfectly administered state, or an economy purged once and for all of conflict. Our hope rests in Christ. That hope calls us into the world's needs rather than excusing us from them. The hungry person does not need a lecture about the impermanence of bread instead of a meal.

Let us build enterprises that serve, institutions that can learn, and households that practice generosity. Let us use markets where they coordinate well and improve the conditions under which they operate. Let us hear the worker's complaint, scrutinize concentrated power, and test proposed remedies. Let us receive a true criticism even from a thinker whose final answers we cannot accept.

And let us remember what our accounts cannot calculate. Money may purchase the materials of a shelter. Love tells us why someone ought to be welcomed inside. Money may carry a claim into tomorrow. It cannot redeem yesterday's cruelty. Money may help sustain a life. It cannot become that life's Lord.

The coin will pass through our hands. Our responsibility is what we do while we hold it.
