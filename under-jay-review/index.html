ACT 01

The creek that started it

On an uphill back porch in Marietta, Georgia, Manny Glover watches birds gather at a newly replenished creek: flat, shallow, and surprisingly wide. Small birds drink and bathe. A large bird sails overhead. A question follows: how smart are these creatures—and how could we possibly know?

GloverThey are so cute and pretty and majestic in their small, humble ways.

WatsonThere may be some very capable little minds visiting that creek.

ACT 02

A statistically significant seven-year-old

The conversation turns to the danger of headlines comparing a bird’s performance on one task with a child’s entire mind.

GloverIn recent studies, advances in statistically driven IQ research discovered that Dr. Michael Emanuel Glover is as smart as a seven-year-old!

WatsonOn one narrowly defined task, under controlled conditions, with a sufficiently motivating snack. Researchers caution that these findings cannot yet be generalized to Dr. Glover’s ability to soar on thermals or remember where he hid 4,000 seeds.

GloverDr. Glover is also a human, so his thermal-soaring and 4,000-seed-hiding claims must be treated as claims till further evidence arises.

WatsonThe authors concede that Dr. Glover’s species was inadequately controlled for. His thermal-soaring and 4,000-seed claims remain unverified; his ability to enjoy birds from a porch is supported by self-report.

ACT 03

The discovery, as yet unidentified

The scale of the discovery grows considerably faster than its definition.

GloverHallelujah! We have done it once again, Dr. Watson! Do you know what we have done?

WatsonWe have successfully distinguished a delightful hypothesis from an established fact—and somehow secured another research snack.

GloverNo! Something bigger, bolder, sexier, and crazier!

WatsonHave we made the study of intelligence itself an act of intelligence flirting with itself?

GloverWe have gone where no man has gone before!

WatsonBeyond the final frontier of bird science—into peer-reviewed porch nonsense. Watson, bring the snacks.

GloverA novel PhD dissertation research direction, to be sure.

ACT 04

The greatest dissertation

A title is born. No degree is awarded.

WatsonEpistemology on the Back Porch: A Comparative Study of Avian Intelligence, Human Self-Assessment, and Watson’s Failure to Appreciate the Magnitude of the Discovery. Dissertation status: awaiting identification of the discovery.

GloverYou’ve just entitled the greatest dissertation in Southern Polytechnic, now Kennesaw State University, history! We’re well on our way to being small-time economics and biology influencers, while chess remains optional and on the table.

WatsonOur research program now spans economics, biology, and the allocation of scarce porch snacks among competing birds. Chess remains optional and on the table—where a blue jay is reviewing our opening.

BACK ON EARTH

What we actually
know about birds.

The comedy began with a serious question: how do you measure a mind that cannot explain itself in human language?

01 / INTELLIGENCE

More than one way to be clever.

Memory, learning, social recognition, self-control, and flexible problem solving are different abilities. There is no universally accepted bird IQ scoreboard, and no defensible single “smartest bird.” Corvids and parrots repeatedly stand out in research, but results belong to particular species, individuals, and tasks.

In some experimental tasks, birds match abilities seen in primates. That does not make them equivalent to a human child—or a chimpanzee—in every respect. Research overview ↗

02 / THE LOCAL MINDS

Crows remember faces.

Researchers wearing a distinctive mask captured and released American crows. The birds later scolded the dangerous face; responses also spread to birds that had not been captured. Changing who wore the mask helped separate facial recognition from recognition of a particular person’s body.

This supports lasting recognition and social learning, not mind-reading or a crow conspiracy. Original social-learning study ↗

03 / SMALL BRAINS

Memory with feathers.

Black-capped chickadees produced distinctive hippocampal activity patterns during individual food-caching events; those patterns reappeared during retrieval. Your likely Marietta chickadee is the Carolina chickadee, a different species.

Bird forebrains can pack neurons densely. Brain weight alone misses much of the story; neuron numbers alone still cannot give an intelligence score. Memory study ↗ · Neuron study ↗

04 / WORLD SPECIALISTS

Tools, labels, and hidden lunches.

Some New Caledonian crows assembled compound tools. Captive rooks dropped stones into water to reach floating food. Certain corvid species have been studied for food-cache memory, sensitivity to observers, and future-oriented choices; the findings and interpretations differ by experiment.

Alex the African grey learned labels and answered questions about quantities and relations such as same/different. Extensive training and individual differences matter. Compound tools ↗ · Rook experiment ↗ · Corvid research ↗ · Alex ↗

HOW TO TEST AN IDEA

“It failed this task” is evidence.
“This bird is dumb” is a leap.

  1. Learning: does performance improve with experience?
  2. Memory: what survives a delay or distraction?
  3. Flexibility: can the bird switch when the old solution fails?
  4. Transfer: can it solve a new version with unfamiliar objects?
  5. Controls: could smell, accidental cues, training, or the experimenter explain success?

A bird that refuses a puzzle may be scared, unmotivated, or unable to handle the objects. Fair comparisons account for vision, movement, prior experience, and rewards. Use multiple individuals and tasks, report uncertainty, and look for independent replication. A “seven-year-old” headline usually describes a comparison on a narrow test, not an entire mind.

Your porch can be an observatory.

Watch without interfering: who arrives first, who follows, which approaches recur, and what changes after an interruption? Record what happened separately from your explanation. Repeated visits may suggest learning, but appearance alone may not tell you whether you saw the same individual.

A distant soaring bird could be a red-tailed hawk or a turkey vulture; the original sighting remains unidentified. Turkey vultures often hold their wings in a shallow V and rock as they soar. Cornell identification help ↗
