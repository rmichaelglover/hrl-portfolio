Rancid Randi · Rapid study

The pawn reaches c7; the rook answers

The most satisfying turn comes in the ten-minute game against Aarkom. Manny has Black in a Scandinavian Defense. White’s queen travels repeatedly, while the d-pawn presses forward. At move nine, 9.dxc7 plants a pawn on the seventh rank, one square from promotion. The rook slides across with 9...Rc8, and after 10.b4 Rxc7 the dangerous passer is gone.

White pushes again with 11.b5. The rook dives down the open c-file: 11...Rxc2! White’s queen captures it, and Black’s queen takes the queen in return: 12.Qxc2 Qxc2. The exchange is dramatic because the rook is offered to remove the queen; the annotated study marks the resulting position as decisively favorable to Black. From there, the queenside pieces and the remaining queen do the work. The game ends after 26...e5, with Black’s material advantage intact.

There is no need to turn the game into a flawless legend. The study’s own engine notes call several of Manny’s moves inaccurate, including the rook invasion itself, and identify stronger alternatives. That makes the chapter more useful: the win came through a sharp practical sequence, while the annotations show where clearer play was available. A good review keeps both truths on the board.

What the two clocks teach

Bullet rewards quick pattern recognition, but speed does not excuse skipping the opponent’s threats. Rapid gives a player more room to calculate—and gives a reviewer more evidence to explain why a plan worked, where it wobbled, and what could be improved. In this small sample, the quick arena is a hard record; the longer game is the instructive recovery.
