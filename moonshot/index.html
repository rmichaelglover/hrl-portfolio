The Sugar Mountain Moonshot

A campaign promise, a stack of cups, one very ambitious surveyor—and the geometry of a sugar mountain that refuses to stay small.

Michael Emanuel Glover · Originating idea, campaign joke, and surveyor candidacy
Developed in dialogue with Codex · calculations, source-checking, and page

The proposal

Build it. Let them come get Moon Diabetes.

The Moonshot begins with a deliberately ridiculous civic promise: take sugar from an imagined Jovian Candyland and build a mountain tall enough to reach the Moon. Michael Emanuel Glover has already voted for himself as surveyor—an indispensable appointment for a project whose first engineering milestone is deciding how wide “the mountain” should be.

The central correction is simple: once the mountain’s shape is chosen, its volume is determined by its height and slope. There is no fixed cup allowance. The cups are a unit for describing volume; the mountain gets to be as enormous as the geometry demands.

A 45° cone with its tip at the Moon’s average distance has a base about 768,800 km wide and contains about 5.95 × 1016 km³ of sugar.
01 · Geometry, with the dial turned up

Choose the slope. The mountain follows.

Use a simple cone as the idealized shape. Let h be the Earth-to-Moon center distance and let θ be the angle between the cone’s side and its flat base. The base radius is r = h / tan θ; the volume is V = πr²h / 3.

r = h / tan(θ)
diameter = 2r
V = π r² h / 3
cups = 10⁹ V[km³] / 0.000236588[m³]

At 45°, the radius equals the height: the cone’s base is about 768,800 km wide. Make the slope shallower and the mountain spreads wider and holds more sugar. Drag the control to explore.

02 · Does adding sugar make the Moon easier to reach?

Technically yes. Practically: planetary.

The gravity idea

Newton’s force law says F = GMEMM / r². If Earth gains mass ΔM and the Earth–Moon separation stays fixed, the attraction scales by 1 + ΔM/ME. Add mass equal to the Moon’s and the force rises by about 1.23%; add one Earth mass and it doubles.

That is a real mathematical direction for the idea: extra mass attracts the Moon. But the Moon is already in orbit, so adding that much mass changes the orbit and the whole Earth–Moon system. It does not act like a convenient ladder.

Scale check

Earth’s mass is about 5.9722×1024 kg; the Moon’s is about 7.346×1022 kg. A Moon-mass of sugar would be roughly 1.23% of Earth’s present mass. Doubling Earth’s mass would require another Earth mass of sugar.

The pictured reach-the-Moon cone is a volume model, not a load-bearing design. Sugar cannot remain a narrow mountain at that scale; gravity would compress and redistribute it long before a summit reached lunar distance.

Moonshot reading: the extra gravity can increase attraction in the equation, but the amount needed to matter turns “add sugar” into “rebuild the mass budget of the Earth–Moon system.” Our campaign keeps the ambition and lets the surveyor handle the fine print.
03 · The sugar source, on the map of imagination

Odo, Candyland, and a small astronomy correction.

For this story, Odo / Candyland is our invented sugar-rich moon in Jupiter’s system. Its crystalline ridges are the origin point for the campaign’s interplanetary supply chain.

One real-world naming correction keeps the fiction neatly labeled: Titania is a real moon of Uranus, not another name for a Jovian moon. So the working map keeps “Odo / Candyland” as our fictional location and reserves “Titania” for Uranus’s largest moon.

Moving material from a Jovian moon to Earth would also be a formidable transportation problem. The thought experiment grants the sugar delivery and asks what follows from the geometry and gravity.

04 · Notebook correction

What the first rough answers got wrong.

The 1.62-billion-cup number had no reach-the-Moon derivation.

The earlier exchange treated a made-up volume of about 384,000 m³ as if it were the required mountain. If that volume were forced into a cone 384,400 km tall, its base would be only about 6 cm across—a needle-thin mathematical shape, not the broad 45° mountain in the campaign illustration.

The earlier “1.1 m” base-width answer was also an arithmetic error. The corrected cone equation gives approximately 6 cm under that artificial volume constraint. The interactive calculator now starts from the actual chosen slope and height, computes the required volume, then converts that volume into cups.
