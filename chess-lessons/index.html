Lecture One · Manny Glover

Memorization is key.
Compression is more key.

Remember the examples. Learn what they have in common. Check what changes.

From a move to a pattern

Memorization gives you a concrete example: “The rook went to e8 and delivered mate.” Compression gives you a reusable explanation: “The king is trapped behind its own pawns, and a rook can attack along the back rank.”

That explanation travels. Move the rook to another file, change the colors, or reach the position through a different opening, and you may still recognize the same idea.

A useful pattern preserves what makes the position work, while leaving out details that do not matter for that decision.

Compression can also throw away something decisive. An extra defender, an escape square, or a countercheck may change everything. Recognition suggests a move; calculation checks it.

Try the pattern

White to move. White has a king on g1 and a rook on e1. Black has a king on g8 and pawns on f7, g7, and h7.

Black at the top · White at the bottom

Which move delivers checkmate?

Show the compressed explanation

Re8#. The rook checks along rank 8. Squares f8 and h8 remain under the rook’s attack. Black’s own pawns occupy f7, g7, and h7, so the king cannot escape. There is no other Black piece to capture the rook or block the check.

If the rook started on d1 instead, Rd8# would apply the same pattern. If Black had an available escape square on h7, we would have to calculate again.

What k-means can contribute

K-means groups numerical examples around cluster centers. In a chess study tool, we could describe positions using material, pawn structure, king safety, open files, and piece activity, then group similar examples.

This is a proposed training method: show real positions from a group, explain their common ideas, and practice the cases where you make mistakes. A cluster center is an average of features; it need not describe a legal chess position. Use actual games as examples.

Similarity is sensitive to how we choose and scale features. Two positions can look similar to a clustering algorithm while a single tactical detail changes the best move. An engine can help check the examples, and a human explanation can make the patterns useful.

Preparing for a stronger opponent

  1. Collect examples. Study your games and your opponent’s public games.
  2. Find recurring structures. Look for positions you are likely to reach.
  3. Compress the lesson. Write a short explanation of the plans, targets, and dangers.
  4. Test the boundaries. Change a defender, a pawn, or whose turn it is. Does the plan still work?
  5. Practice without hints. Choose and calculate your move, then check the result.

Examples → patterns → candidate moves → verification.

The goal is faster recognition and better decisions. Whether this improves results against strong opponents needs testing on new positions and games; grouping examples alone does not establish that benefit.

The same idea in music

A melody has individual notes. A motif lets you recognize a phrase across different instruments, keys, or rhythms. Our chess track keeps the moves as individual events while using repeated harmony and rhythm to make a musical whole.
