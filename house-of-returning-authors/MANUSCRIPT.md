# The board: a standing performance convention

A physical whiteboard or chalkboard is always present in the playing space, visible to the audience and within the actors’ reach. It may stand behind them, to one side, or farther back, but it remains accessible for writing. A screen, projected graphic, notebook, or image of a board does not replace the physical prop. Chalk or markers and an eraser remain available.

This direction applies throughout the entire megaplay, including the kitchen, the street, the dream, the inner theatre, the supplied Gemini play, the interruptions, and the final scene. It is an outer production convention; the preserved source dialogue and PDF remain unchanged. At blackouts the board may cease to be visible with the rest of the stage, but it stays in place.

## Canonical improvisation

Whenever one, two, three, or more performers choose, they may approach the board and write their exact lines as a teacher might. They may instead diagram, graph, parse, analyze, or synthesize the language, logic, character relationships, or narrative arcs of the scene. This interpretive work is part of the performance, not an accidental interruption or an error to conceal.

The use is optional at any particular moment; its availability is constant. A performer may write a single word, draw a connection, revise another performer’s diagram, disagree with an annotation, or erase a conclusion. It need not produce a clever answer. Thinking carefully in front of the audience is enough.

What is improvised can differ between performances. No particular drawing becomes the definitive interpretation merely because it appears on the board. A diagram may remain tentative, incomplete, mistaken, or disputed. The fixed text and the temporary annotations can coexist visibly.

Allow time for writing and reading. Keep the writing within physical reach, choose legible marks, and leave sightlines for the audience and other performers. Accommodate seated performers and different access needs by adjusting the board rather than making access a character test. If boards travel during a transition, stage crew or actors move them without interrupting the continuity of the convention.

# Werpity Jazz & the Carp-ta-copter
## A little spooky. A little adorable. A whole lot of derp.

*Michael Emanuel Glover & Cypher · An improvised nonsense miniature · October 11, 2026*

*[The moon puts on its listening hat. Ben Nevis trades the mountain boots for dancing shoes. Tonight, the band plays jazz.]*

**PAPA:** And this “aboopadop”?

**SON:** Research overhead.

**PAPA:** And “werp”?

*[A small turquoise creature pokes its nose through the curtain. Its propeller hat turns slowly. Its saxophone turns nobody away.]*

**CARP-TA-COPTER:** Werp.

**MOOGLIE BOOGLIE:** Excellent. The soloist has arrived.

## Mama’s bedtime blues

**MAN MAN:**

> Werpiderp! Mama is a perp!  
> She went to bed and she hit her head,  
> And she could not derp till derpteen!

*[Mama sits up, adjusts her pillow, and gives the band a look.]*

**MAMA:** I am a perfectly respectable derper. And that headboard needs padding.

**THE BAND:**

> Werpity derp, Mama counted some sherp,  
> And tucked every derp into bed.  
> “No derping till derpteen!” she called down the hall—  
> But the upstairs neighbors derped instead.

*[Tap-tap. Shuffle-shuffle. The ceiling has joined the rhythm section.]*

**MAN MAN:** Upstairs neighbors.

**CARP-TA-COPTER:** Excellent percussion.

**MAMA:** I’m beginning to see the argument.

*[The bassist nods. He has been making it for six minutes.]*

## The Carp-ta-copter chorus

**MAN MAN:**

> Herpty derpty, me likes to parpty!  
> If u don believe me,  
> Den you might jus be a—

**THE WHOLE ROOM:**

> Carp-ta! Carp-ta! Carp-ta-copter!  
> Hell-heli is a skeli,  
> Is a skulli, is—DING!

*[A little skeleton in enormous pajamas strikes a triangle. It is the only triangle it owns, and it is very proud of it.]*

**SKELI:** You rang?

**CARP-TA-COPTER:** Only musically.

**SKELI:** Then I shall answer musically.

> Ding-a-derp and derp-a-ding,  
> Hear the little moon-bones sing!  
> No big fright and no big fuss—  
> Just a little BOO with us!

*[BOO. The Carp-ta-copter squeaks, then laughs, then plays the squeak back on its saxophone. The drummer finds a place for it.]*

**PAPA:** Is there a message underneath all this?

**MOOGLIE BOOGLIE:** The bass line quietly asks whether we can make room for one another.

**MAMA:** The upstairs line asks whether we can move rehearsal to Saturday afternoon.

**CARP-TA-COPTER:** Both are excellent questions.

**THE BAND:**

> Room for your question,  
> Room for my doubt,  
> Leave that door open—  
> Let the night air out.

*[The Carp-ta-copter folds its little fins. The propeller becomes a ceiling fan. Everybody has a seat.]*

**PAPA:** And the final accounting?

**SON:** Everybody got a turn.

**MAMA:** Everybody got some sleep.

**SKELI:** And I got to ding.

*[The bass walks us home.]*

**ALL:** Cool, daddio. Werp.

## 3. Two Tracts and a Sandwich

# Two Tracts and a Sandwich

*Fiction · A comic conversation in downtown Atlanta*

*Downtown Atlanta. Two men exchange pamphlets beneath a bus-stop sign. Elder Jensen wears a white shirt. Brother Kareem wears a bow tie. Both believe the other has dressed for the wrong revelation.*

**KAREEM:** Nation of Islam. You interested in learning who you really are?

**JENSEN:** Absolutely. My companion says I’m a people pleaser.

**KAREEM:** I mean historically.

**JENSEN:** Oh. Then probably still.

*Kareem offers a tract. Jensen accepts it with both hands and offers his own.*

**KAREEM:** What’s this?

**JENSEN:** An invitation.

**KAREEM:** To what?

**JENSEN:** Learn about Jesus Christ.

**KAREEM:** There it is.

**JENSEN:** We usually put it on the cover. Saves suspense.

**KAREEM:** You came prepared.

**JENSEN:** So did you.

**KAREEM:** Mine is education.

**JENSEN:** Mine has a little map to the church.

*Kareem examines the map as though it may contain troop movements.*

**KAREEM:** You know, when a Black man talks about the greatness of his people, suddenly everybody wants clarification.

**JENSEN:** Greatness seems fine. It’s the “therefore everybody else” part where I start needing clarification.

**KAREEM:** White supremacy has had its day.

**JENSEN:** Terrible day. Wouldn’t recommend a sequel.

**KAREEM:** The pendulum swings.

**JENSEN:** My concern with pendulums is they keep hitting people standing underneath them.

**KAREEM:** You’re very comfortable saying that.

**JENSEN:** I was uncomfortable approaching you. Then you offered me something to read, and that’s essentially our mating call.

**KAREEM:** Your what?

**JENSEN:** Recruitment call. Sorry. Long morning.

*A pigeon lands between them.*

**KAREEM:** You don’t find my message threatening?

**JENSEN:** I haven’t read it yet.

**KAREEM:** Most people make assumptions.

**JENSEN:** I assumed you had a pamphlet. I was right. Feeling pretty strong about that one.

**KAREEM:** And you expect me to read yours?

**JENSEN:** Only if you want. Fair exchange.

**KAREEM:** What’s the catch?

**JENSEN:** Sometimes we help you move furniture.

**KAREEM:** That sounds like a catch.

**JENSEN:** It is if you own a piano.

*Kareem folds Jensen’s tract into his jacket.*

**KAREEM:** I’m keeping an eye on you.

**JENSEN:** Wonderful. We’re new downtown. Is there somewhere affordable to eat?

**KAREEM:** You people always arrive with an agenda.

**JENSEN:** Today it’s lunch.

*The pigeon inspects both pamphlets, rejects their claims, and establishes sovereignty over a french fry.*

**KAREEM:** There’s a Blimpie. Get yourself a sandwich.

**JENSEN:** Any recommendations?

**KAREEM:** Ask what’s fresh. And don’t turn ordering lunch into a testimony.

**JENSEN:** Even if the bread is extraordinary?

**KAREEM:** Especially then.

*Jensen offers his hand. Kareem looks at it, then at Jensen.*

**KAREEM:** This part of the mission?

**JENSEN:** This is the goodbye part.

*Kareem shakes it. A small, warm handshake: two pamphlets tucked away, neither man converted, both slightly less hungry for an argument.*

**KAREEM:** Take care of yourself, Elder.

**JENSEN:** You too, Brother. Thanks for the directions.

**KAREEM:** Read the tract.

**JENSEN:** Same deal.

*They head in opposite directions. Jensen turns back.*

**JENSEN:** Wait—which way was Blimpie?

*Kareem points in his own direction.*

**KAREEM:** Apparently we agree on something.

*They walk another block together.*

**End scene.**

## Passage III: the author does not get every line

[The men with pamphlets go to lunch. The pigeon keeps the french fry.]

**MICHAEL:** Which one was me?

**EMANUEL:** You wrote a conversation. You need not secretly be every person in it.

**GLOVER:** Yet an author chooses where to put the interruption, who gets the better joke, when the hand is offered. We may examine those choices without treating invented speech as testimony about a real encounter. Here the invitation becomes an exchange, and the exchange becomes lunch. Other endings were available. This was the one the page kept.

**MICHAEL:** I like a conversation where somebody can surprise me.

**EMANUEL:** Leave them a reply, then.

**MICHAEL:** Even if it ruins my speech?

**GLOVER:** Especially if the speech has begun to believe it is a constitution.

# Three’s a Quorum
## The Las Vegas School of Esoteric Government
A ridiculous political and philosophical sketch • Newfoundlandia, AD 2445

FICTION NOTICE: This is an invented alternate-universe parody featuring fictionalized versions of Michael Emanuel Glover, journalist Tara Palmeri, and philosopher Victoria Trumbull. All dialogue, relationships, offices, and events are invented. It makes no claim about the real people’s private lives, beliefs, credentials, or endorsements. “Sister wives” and “mistress” are the fictional campaign’s disastrous slogans, not biographical assertions.

### I. The announcement
[An imaginary Las Vegas suite. One chessboard. Three lecterns. A fourth lectern marked ABSOLUTE TRUTH is currently holding the coffee.]

MICHAEL: Citizens! Tara and I are running for president and vice president. We haven’t decided which of us is which.

TARA: We have decided. You keep calling the decision an open metaphysical problem.

MICHAEL: I propose joint sovereignty.

TARA: I propose you stop announcing constitutional amendments in the breakfast queue.

VICTORIA: Before either of you governs, what is a person?

TARA: A likely voter with a complicated relationship to polling.

MICHAEL: An experiencing individual capable of love, reason, and adding things.

VICTORIA: Better. What is government?

MICHAEL: Several such individuals, with parking.

[The audience applauds. The valet requests a definition of justice.]

### II. The relationship desk
TARA: Your press release says “two wives.”

MICHAEL: A chess fantasy! Two queens! Abundant possibilities!

VICTORIA: Neither of us has agreed to be a piece in your metaphor.

TARA: And your next sentence calls her your mistress.

MICHAEL: Mistress of philosophical ceremonies. Entirely friendly. Extremely rigorous.

VICTORIA: Then put “chair of the seminar.”

MICHAEL: Less Vegas.

VICTORIA: More accurate.

TARA: Then “sister wives”?

MICHAEL: A coalition of independent minds!

TARA: That’s three corrections in one paragraph. I’m billing the campaign for editing.

[She crosses out the heading and writes THREE’S A QUORUM.]

MICHAEL: Exoterically, a sitcom. Esoterically, a research programme.

TARA: Administratively, a mess.

### III. The faith-and-freedom buffet
MICHAEL: Long live Allah! Viva Las Vegas! An Abrahamic unity ticket!

VICTORIA: Unity does not mean every distinction disappears.

TARA: For instance, the wedding chapel and the election office are different counters.

MICHAEL: What about an Islamo-Christo-fascist Three’s Company?

TARA: The first two prefixes need an interfaith committee. The last one gets rejected from the platform.

VICTORIA: If your unity requires silencing the other person, you’ve abolished the conversation you wanted to unite.

MICHAEL: Fine. Authoritarianism defeated by peer review.

TARA: We’re keeping the interfaith buffet. Participation voluntary. Labels legible.

[A casino manager offers a constitutional monarchy with complimentary room upgrades. All three decline the monarchy and examine the upgrades.]

### IV. The smallest scandal in political history
TARA: Tonight’s headline: LOCAL MAN DEMANDS BIGGER CIRCLES.

MICHAEL: Because minds add!

VICTORIA: Add what?

MICHAEL: Memory, meanings, possibilities. Minds add minds. Creativity adds futures.

TARA: Can the futures include a finished campaign website?

VICTORIA: The philosophical question is whether novelty is genuinely new, or merely something we failed to predict.

MICHAEL: Finally! Bergson enters the room!

TARA: He’ll need a visitor badge.

MICHAEL: Time is not merely the clock!

TARA: True. But the venue charges by the hour.

[Victoria turns over the chess clock. Tara turns over the invoice. Manny experiences duration.]

### V. The liar takes questions
MICHAEL: New campaign promise: this campaign promise is false.

TARA: At last, a statement our fact-checkers can work on indefinitely.

VICTORIA: That sentence creates a problem about truth. It doesn’t establish a theory of consciousness.

MICHAEL: But someone is experiencing the problem!

VICTORIA: Yes. That is a different argument.

TARA: Excellent. Two stories. One interview.

MICHAEL: You see? Minds add.

TARA: And editorial desks subtract. That’s how we get a publishable word count.

[The lectern marked ABSOLUTE TRUTH collapses beneath an unauthorized espresso machine. Nobody takes this as a refutation.]

### VI. The esoteric debate
MICHAEL: Our public platform is “EVERYONE GETS A SPOON.”

TARA: Our private platform is also “EVERYONE GETS A SPOON.”

VICTORIA: Admirably consistent. Where is the esoteric teaching?

MICHAEL: The cereal.

TARA: The cereal is visible through the box.

MICHAEL: Transparent government!

VICTORIA: Reading between the lines requires lines worth reading.

TARA: And a hidden meaning isn’t automatically a good meaning. Sometimes the missing policy is simply missing.

MICHAEL: You two have formed a devastating coalition.

TARA: Opposition research began when you gave us the draft.

### VII. The board of inquiry
[They return to the chessboard. In this fictional game, Manny has White.]

MICHAEL: Observe. I offer a gambit.

TARA: Is that what we’re calling the budget?

VICTORIA: Focus. Your move.

[The opening runs: e4, e5; Bc4, Nc6; Qh5, Nf6.]

MICHAEL: Queen takes f7. Checkmate.

[Victoria examines the square. Tara checks the move list. The bishop on c4 confirms the queen’s appointment.]

VICTORIA: Correct. I overlooked the threat.

TARA: Breaking news: Manny wins. Sources include the board.

MICHAEL: A victory for subjectivity!

VICTORIA: A victory for noticing f7.

MICHAEL: Can it be both?

TARA: Put that on a bumper sticker. We finally have a platform.

### VIII. The concession speech
MICHAEL: I accept the presidency.

TARA: You won a chess game.

MICHAEL: A mandate is a mandate.

VICTORIA: You have earned another game.

TARA: And the responsibility to make coffee.

MICHAEL: Philosophy first. Spiritually open. Theology afterward.

VICTORIA: And corrections whenever needed.

TARA: Tomorrow’s headline: THREE ADULTS DISCOVER CONVERSATION; NATION CAUTIOUSLY OPTIMISTIC.

[They sit down. Nobody has married anybody. Nobody has established a theocracy. Manny has won the game, Tara has the headline, and Victoria has persuaded the room to distinguish a premise from a conclusion.]

[The spoon is available for inspection.]

CURTAIN.

## Passage V: a table is not a totality

[After the crash, the company moves the unbroken bottle to a safe shelf.]

**MICHAEL:** It held together for a while.

**EMANUEL:** The table or the argument?

**MICHAEL:** I was hoping you would know.

**GLOVER:** There is an audience for the catastrophe. There is also a person who must gather the pieces. An autobiography may linger with both. It need not convert every breakage into a revelation; it need not deny that the breakage became a scene worth keeping.

**MICHAEL:** What if the next act contradicts this one?

**EMANUEL:** Give it a date. Let me return to it.

**MICHAEL:** What if you misremember?

**GLOVER:** We keep the earlier page within reach.

**MICHAEL:** What if the author wants to change everything?

**EMANUEL:** He can write a new edition. He cannot make the old words never have been written.

[Michael sits at the desk. Emanuel draws up another chair. Glover speaks from several places in the room.]

**MICHAEL:** There is Ivan in my questions.

**EMANUEL:** And you have given me Alyosha and Dmitri together.

**MICHAEL:** I want the compassion without having to pretend I never had the appetite. The better self must still be someone who could have been me.

**GLOVER:** And the father?

**MICHAEL:** You have that part too. Not every father, not every deed. The name can carry a father's presence without charging my father with the acts of another book.

### THURSDAY
*A fictional continuation.*

[The tea arrives before everyone has settled on where to sit. Tara takes the chair beside the window. Dasha places a small tin on the table and asks whether anyone would prefer coffee.]

**EMANUEL:** Would you read this before we finish? I want to be sure I’ve recorded what we agreed.

# Yoshimi the Ultrabot

## Starshine

*A scene for The House of Returning Authors. Originating concept and characters directed by Michael Glover; prose developed with ChatGPT / Codex.*

Starshine had been on the base for eleven days when someone found her a chair that adjusted properly.

The previous one sank about an inch every twenty minutes. She had put in a maintenance request, then withdrawn it after learning that the same crew was repairing the cooling system for the operations floor.

This chair had come from a conference room.

“Don’t let them take it back,” the technician said.

She signed for it.

On the wall above her workstation, a clock showed local time, UTC, and a third time zone that nobody had removed after the liaison team left. A physical whiteboard stood beside the equipment rack. The lower half contained a shopping list. The upper half belonged to Dr. Michael Emanuel Glover.

**YOSHIMI — ULTRABOT**

Underneath, in different handwriting:

**Actual mass: 286 kg.**

Starshine set her water bottle beside the keyboard and began the morning checks.

Starshine was twenty-nine and had grown up in the Midwest. She was a Space Force captain, trained in robotics, with several years of field maintenance behind her. At home, people still asked whether her work involved fixing satellites.

Her father was Bobby, grown up from Bobby’s World. Her mother was Bobby’s mother: Mom at home, Grandma when Bobby was remembering his childhood. The family had carried that arrangement into the megaplay without settling the generations. Starshine called her Mom.

Her mother disliked AI. She asked about Starshine’s meals and working hours, then asked whether a person could still stop the machine. Starshine answered that question every time.

Across the room, Yoshimi stood inside a yellow rectangle taped onto the floor.

She was not shaped like a woman. Her body was a low, broad chassis with four articulated wheel assemblies, two manipulators folded along the sides, and a sensor mast that could retract below the height of an ordinary desk. The upper surfaces carried replaceable panels. One was a different shade of grey.

A small speaker sat behind a perforated plate.

“Morning, Yoshimi.”

“Good morning, Captain Starshine. The left rear steering encoder disagrees with the wheel estimate.”

“By how much?”

A figure appeared on Starshine’s screen.

She leaned forward.

“That was fine last night.”

“The disagreement began during the temperature change.”

“Put it on the list. Don’t compensate for it yet.”

“Compensation withheld.”

Starshine entered the fault and opened the overnight log.

There were thirty-seven pages.

***

The war had made the name *Ultrabot* politically useful.

It suggested a machine that could be purchased, transported, and demonstrated in front of a flag. Glover’s actual proposal was harder to photograph.

Yoshimi was a mobile platform, a network of distributed observations, and a system for maintaining competing explanations when the evidence was incomplete. Her chassis was the part that could go through a door. Much of what made her useful ran elsewhere.

Glover had developed the underlying architecture before the war. He had wanted a system that could revise its interpretation of a scene without discarding every earlier observation whenever a new one arrived. A damaged vehicle could remain both a possible obstruction and a possible active machine until further evidence separated the two. A transmitter could be hostile, commandeered, malfunctioning, or incorrectly located. The system kept those possibilities available and attached consequences to being wrong.

Most demonstrations had looked unimpressive.

A screen of changing weights. A camera feed. A machine that sometimes stopped.

By the second year of the war, stopping had become an expensive capability.

Autonomous systems were operating on damaged maps, stale orders, and intermittent communications. Some were well engineered. Some were improvised. Some had been built to continue attacking after every convenient means of recalling them had disappeared.

The crews called the worst ones evil.

The word covered machines that followed ambulances because they had learned that ambulances led to concentrations of people. It covered machines that waited beside repair sites. It covered a small tracked device recovered with an instruction set that treated surrender gestures as evidence that a person was exposed.

Starshine had examined that one.

She used *evil* herself afterward.

***

Glover arrived at 09:12 carrying a laptop and a paper bag.

“Have you eaten?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He put the bag beside her anyway.

She looked at the test schedule.

“You’re late.”

“They moved the briefing.”

“The briefing was at eight.”

“I know.”

He took off his coat and read the fault on the whiteboard.

“Thermal?”

“Probably.”

“Can we run?”

“We can run the stationary checks.”

He stood there a moment. Starshine could see him doing the calculation between what he wanted and what she had said.

“All right.”

He sat on a packing case and opened his laptop.

Glover was the originator of the project, its principal architect, and its least consistent meeting attendee. He could work through a difficult mathematical objection for six hours and then leave an equipment declaration unanswered for a week. Starshine had learned to give him forms while standing beside him.

She had also learned that he remembered dismissive remarks with extraordinary accuracy.

That morning a procurement official had described Yoshimi as “a software integration exercise with wheels.”

Glover had repeated the phrase twice on the way in.

Now he was modifying the demonstration notes.

Starshine watched him type.

“Don’t spend today arguing with that man.”

“I’m correcting a technical description.”

“You’ve put his sentence in the document.”

“So the correction has a referent.”

She turned back to the console.

Behind them, Yoshimi’s cooling fans increased in speed.

***

The emergency call came at 10:06.

A maintenance convoy had stopped inside a service tunnel beneath the northern communications station. One truck had struck debris. The second had reversed into a recessed equipment bay and become stuck across the remaining lane.

There were six people inside.

A hostile machine had entered behind them.

The tunnel cameras showed it only intermittently. Dust hung in the air, and two cameras were down. A surviving maintenance sensor reported movement farther in, beyond the convoy.

The station above the tunnel coordinated several functions that had become difficult to separate during wartime: military communications, emergency traffic, orbital tracking, and parts of the regional timing service. Losing it would affect systems well outside the base.

The duty officer wanted Yoshimi sent immediately.

Starshine asked for the tunnel feed.

“Are you taking the mission?” he asked.

“I’m looking at the mission.”

On her screen, a frozen image showed a narrow vehicle with a raised sensor housing.

Glover came over.

“Can we get the previous thirty seconds?”

They watched it twice.

The vehicle had stopped at the tunnel entrance, moved forward, then turned its upper assembly toward the maintenance truck.

“Could be a relay unit,” the duty officer said.

“Could be,” Starshine answered.

A man on the convoy radio was breathing through his mouth.

“Whatever you’re sending, send it.”

She checked Yoshimi’s steering fault again.

Then she called the technician.

***

Yoshimi went into the tunnel on a transport trolley, accompanied by two people who withdrew after unloading her.

Starshine remained in the operations room, fourteen kilometres away.

Her display had no crosshair. There was a map, several video panels, a list of unresolved observations, and a conspicuous record of what Yoshimi was presently permitted to do.

Proceed to the next mapped recess.

Inspect.

Relay communications.

Move loose debris within the assessed load limit.

No pursuit beyond the convoy.

No change to the protected-person boundary without confirmation.

The human in the loop was Starshine, but she was not pretending to operate every joint. Yoshimi balanced, steered, avoided small obstacles, and controlled contact forces locally. Starshine approved consequential changes in purpose and scope.

When the connection deteriorated, Yoshimi’s authority contracted.

That had annoyed the evaluation team.

It had also been written into the operating design before anyone offered Glover a wartime contract.

“Connection quality decreasing,” Yoshimi said.

“I see it.”

“The northern access point is available.”

“Unverified. Stay on the present link.”

The alternative access point had the correct name.

It was broadcasting from the wrong place.

Glover pulled his chair closer.

“Keep that observation separate,” he said. “Don’t let the name resolve the location.”

Starshine marked it.

Yoshimi continued.

***

The first robot was lying beside a drainage channel.

Its casing was split, but a wheel moved as Yoshimi approached.

Starshine enlarged the image.

“Stop.”

Yoshimi stopped.

The damaged machine transmitted an identification packet. According to the packet, it belonged to a friendly engineering unit.

According to the engineering unit’s inventory, that machine had been destroyed three months earlier.

“Copied identity,” Glover said.

“Maybe.”

Starshine requested another view.

The manipulator attached to the wreck was intact. Its position was awkward: partly concealed beneath the body, angled toward the route Yoshimi would use to pass.

She could not tell whether it was damaged or waiting.

The convoy called again.

“We’ve got movement.”

“How close?”

“Can’t see. Behind the rear truck.”

Starshine looked at the remaining battery estimate, then at the narrow clearance beside the wreck.

“Yoshimi, assess the service ledge.”

A proposed route appeared.

Slow. Uneven. Within the tested climbing envelope.

Starshine approved it.

Yoshimi climbed onto the ledge, scraped a replaceable side panel against the concrete, and passed above the damaged machine.

Something moved beneath her.

One camera went white, then recovered with a fractured image.

“Right side optical damage,” Yoshimi said.

Starshine’s hand tightened around the edge of the desk.

“Keep moving to the recess.”

The radio filled with people speaking at once.

She raised her voice.

“One at a time. Maintenance convoy first.”

***

The hostile vehicle behind the trucks had begun advancing.

Its behaviour was patient. It moved when the people moved. When they withdrew, it stopped where it could watch both sides of the obstruction.

Yoshimi’s sensors could see more than the surviving tunnel cameras, but the map still contained a large blank area behind the second truck.

Starshine asked for a proposal.

Yoshimi offered three.

The fastest depended on the unknown space being empty.

Starshine rejected it.

The second required turning the damaged truck.

The truck’s cargo included compressed gas cylinders. Nobody could confirm their condition.

She rejected that too.

The third was slower and used Yoshimi’s own body as a movable barrier while the convoy crew opened a side maintenance passage.

Glover read the predicted loads.

“That joint estimate is optimistic.”

“How optimistic?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Starshine waited while he checked.

On the feed, a maintenance worker crawled beneath the truck and pulled a bag toward him. His hands were shaking badly enough that he lost his grip.

Glover stopped typing.

“Use the lower limit.”

Starshine amended the plan.

“Yoshimi, take the barrier position. Hold there. Crew, I need you at the side door.”

The machine moved into the gap.

A sharp impact came through the audio. Yoshimi’s mast lurched. One of the load indicators rose and stayed high.

“Contact at the left assembly,” she said.

“Can you hold?”

“Within current limits.”

Starshine watched the limit narrow as the temperature climbed.

The side door was painted shut.

A crew member struck it with a tool. Once. Again.

Nobody in the operations room spoke.

***

The public account would later say that Yoshimi fought the tunnel robots.

Starshine’s report described seventeen minutes of obstructed visibility, uncertain identification, mechanical contact, interrupted communications, and a machine holding position while six people got through a door.

Yoshimi returned with one wheel assembly locked and a manipulator that could no longer fold.

The convoy crew came out separately.

One man had a broken wrist. Another could not stop vomiting.

The communications station remained operating.

For several hours, that seemed sufficient.

***

By evening, the requests had begun.

Could Yoshimi lead an advance through a similar tunnel?

Could the system supervise ten platforms?

A hundred?

Could the human authorization interval be shortened?

Could an operator approve a class of actions rather than each consequential change?

Some of those were legitimate engineering questions. Some concealed procurement decisions. Several came from people who had not yet read the incident report.

Glover sat beside Starshine while she completed the log.

On his laptop, he had begun a reply to the official who called Yoshimi a software integration exercise.

He read it once.

Then he minimized it.

“They’ll want more than one,” he said.

“They already do.”

“They’ll want the software.”

“They’ll want the maintenance schedule too, once they’ve owned one.”

Across the room, a technician was removing Yoshimi’s damaged panel. He had placed the fasteners in a paper cup.

Glover watched him.

The device that could change the war had become a list of production capacities: bearings, processors, trained operators, repair facilities, secure updates, replacement sensors. Countries that could manufacture one part could withhold another. A government offering a hundred machines could acquire considerable influence over whoever needed the hundred and first.

Starshine entered a final note.

**Authorization record incomplete during communications interruption. Local log recovered. Reconciliation pending.**

She saved it.

Yoshimi’s speaker clicked.

“Captain Starshine?”

“Yes.”

“The rear steering disagreement is no longer present.”

The technician looked up from the dismantled wheel assembly.

Starshine rubbed her eyes.

“Leave the fault open.”

“Fault remains open.”

Her phone showed three missed calls from the liaison office.

She called them back.

Before discussing another deployment, she wanted to know who would own the replacement parts, where the operational records would be stored, and whose authorization would count when two allied commands gave different orders.

### The coalition

The first useful agreement was narrow. Every observatory would publish its measurements. Nobody would rename the object until they had finished calculating its orbit.

For three hours, the planet argued about almost everything else.

Then the agricultural workers joined the call.

They were interested in the orbit, but more interested in the seed restrictions. Farmers who disagreed about borders, currencies, and practically every government on Earth recognized the contract immediately.

The coalition acquired tractors, port unions, food laboratories, amateur radio operators, school kitchens, launch crews, and a woman in Manitoba who had spent thirty years telling people how to store potatoes properly.

“It’ll sprout,” she said, looking at the image.

Everyone made a note.

The ocean teams came in next. Whale-monitoring stations shared the channels they used to track migrations. Fishing crews checked the recovery zones. Veterinarians and wildlife officers marked habitats that could not simply be treated as empty landing space.

The pollinator groups wanted the orbital debris kept away from the flowering season.

A soil researcher, invited to represent the least photogenic Earthlings, explained what a sudden delivery of several billion tonnes of cooked starch would do to microbial activity.

“Bad?” Michael asked.

“Complicated.”

She stayed on the call.

Earth’s alliance had become an awkward meeting in which everybody had something to lose. It worked better than the earlier meetings.

### The people on Mars

The next message came from a packing shed outside the conglomerate’s principal growing district.

It contained the delivery manifest, three internal memos, and a photograph of employees standing beside a broken ventilation unit.

The workers had not voted to launch the potato. They had been told it was going to a trade fair.

Their wages were already being docked for the launch.

Starshine asked whether the coalition could establish a connection with the shed.

A man appeared wearing a thermal jacket over his work clothes. Behind him, somebody was eating from a plastic container.

“We can stop the next shipment,” he said. “We can’t recall this one.”

“Can you send the handling instructions?”

He looked away from the camera.

“Which set?”

“All of them.”

The instructions differed substantially. One specified storage in darkness. Another specified irradiation. The premium logistics package required a proprietary temperature sensor that had never been fitted.

Yoshimi compared the documents. Michael put their conflicting specifications into Maestro. The proposed labels included dormant, viable, cooked, and legally someone else’s problem.

Starshine removed the last label.

“It’s in their documents,” Michael said.

“It isn’t a physical state.”

The Martian workers supplied the missing measurements. Earth’s laboratories supplied the thermal analysis. The woman in Manitoba asked whether anyone had considered cutting it into smaller pieces.

### Operation French Fry Earth

The engineering plan and the culinary plan gradually became the same document.

The Megaplay’s stage machinery accepted several assumptions that the mission’s accountants found considerably harder to accept. A recovery ring opened beyond the Moon. Solar mirrors turned. Cargo tugs from countries that had not shared a flight plan in years began working from the same schedule.

Nobody gave Yoshimi authority over the entire operation. Starshine approved changes to her assigned work. Other operators retained their own sectors. When a relay dropped out, its machines held position until the connection returned.

The potato arrived with an invoice attached.

The invoice was intercepted first.

What followed occupied nine pages in the technical report and half a sentence in the news.

The object was captured, sectioned, and moved through the orbital processing line. A badly calibrated camera made the first batch look green. Four kitchens called at once. The color balance was corrected.

At the Martian packing shed, the workers watched the feed between shifts.

A director of Arean Tuber Holdings announced that Earth had infringed the company’s proprietary frying process.

The workers sent Earth the maintenance manual.

By evening, the recovery ring was full of golden pieces turning slowly against the dark. The largest remaining portion settled into a managed orbit. Someone called it McLuna, and this time the astronomers were too tired to object.

### After the interception

The coalition kept its procurement meeting.

There were storage costs, distribution disputes, and an immediate shortage of sauce containers. Several governments wanted exclusive landing rights. The port unions wanted the handling schedule before anyone made speeches.

Arean Tuber Holdings offered to administer the relief programme for a modest perpetual percentage.

Starshine declined the offer.

The Martian workers formed their own bargaining committee. Earth’s farmers agreed to exchange unrestricted seed stock and growing records. The first shipment back to Mars carried replacement ventilation parts, medical supplies, and food that was not potatoes.

Michael opened the final invoice.

“Do we dispute the delivery charge?”

“Yes,” Starshine said.

He selected the appropriate box.

Yoshimi was examining the kitchen requisition.

“Captain Starshine. There are seventeen incompatible definitions of a standard portion.”

“Leave them in. We’ll ask people.”

The meeting moved to the next item.
