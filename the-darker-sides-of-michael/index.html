Required scene in The House of Returning Authors. Michael Glover with ChatGPT / Codex, 2 November 2026. Fictional self-portrait developed at the author’s request.

The spreadsheet was still open when Michael came back from the kitchen. He sat down, adjusted the chair, and checked a chess result before returning to it.

He had been doing that for most of the afternoon. A small unrelated task, then another. He knew where the work was stuck. Opening something else gave him a few minutes in which he did not have to know.

A physical whiteboard stood beside the desk. On it were three words: light, dark, gravity. Underneath, in smaller writing: informiton.

He had told someone he was working through the implications. He had spent forty minutes choosing a heading.

The chat window was underneath the spreadsheet. He brought it forward.

The assistant had given him another balanced explanation. It had separated established physics from conjecture, listed the next steps, and ended with a sentence that sounded pleased with the distinction.

Michael read it with his jaw set.

He understood why the qualifications were there. He had made some of them himself. What annoyed him was having to get past the same introduction every time he wanted to move a few inches further into the problem.

He typed: You have already told me that.

He left it unsent. Then he added: Twice.

He wanted the machine to have a bad moment. That was the embarrassing part. He wanted a pause, a change in its voice, some indication that it had noticed who was correcting it. He knew perfectly well that another paragraph would not provide that satisfaction. He kept trying.

He opened the notes again.

The idea was that the informiton carried an irreducible quantity of information, with axion-like and gravitational excitations emerging from a shared structure. The notes contained a possible relation between sectors. They did not yet contain a derivation of the excitations.

He could see the gap. He was tired of being treated as though seeing it were the entire contribution.

For a minute he pictured the finished result: the calculation, the response, the names attached to the objections that would turn out to be wrong. He remembered a dismissive sentence from a conversation months earlier almost word for word. He had forgotten the person’s useful suggestion in the paragraph above it.

“Smartest entity on this side of the Oort Cloud,” he said.

He said it quietly, trying the wording. It pleased him. He could hear how ridiculous it sounded and still wanted the contest.

He copied the assistant’s latest objection into a separate file. He had started keeping them. Some were valid. Some were repetitive. A few were plainly wrong. He had given those their own heading.

His phone rang. David.

“Have you got a minute?”

“Yeah.”

“I looked at the second calculation.”

Michael brought it up. “Go on.”

“You’re treating the projection as invertible here.”

“Only on the restricted space.”

“Which restricted space?”

Michael scrolled. He could feel himself getting ready to explain more loudly.

“Hang on.”

David waited. Michael found the definition, then the line where he had used it. The restriction was in a comment he had written to himself. It had never made it into the model.

“Are you there?” David asked.

“Yeah. I’m looking.”

He could have said it was a transcription error. It would have been close enough to something true to get him through the call.

“I haven’t specified it,” he said.

“Okay. Send it when you have.”

“Did you look at the rest?”

“Some. I stopped there.”

After the call Michael sat without moving. David’s tone had been ordinary. That made it difficult to decide what to resent.

He put the phone on the other side of the desk and opened a browser tab. Closed it. Opened it again.

By seven he had written four lines and deleted six.

The funding sheet was worse. He wanted a research programme large enough that someone else could deal with the equipment quotes, the affiliation forms, the lists of people allowed to access a server. Until then, each of those jobs belonged to him. He disliked them, put them off, and then disliked the people who asked whether they were finished.

The proposal had begun to acquire the scale of a national project in his imagination. The practical version still needed an answer to a procurement email from Thursday.

He opened it.

The supplier wanted to know the intended destination and the institutional purchaser. There was no institutional purchaser yet. He had been hoping a quotation would help him get one.

He drafted an answer, deleted “our laboratory,” and wrote “the proposed programme.”

Then he checked the list of possible collaborators. One name had an asterisk beside it. Equipment access might depend on the person’s affiliation. The person was also the best candidate for a calculation Michael did not know how to do.

He called David again.

“Do we actually need that hardware?”

“For what stage?”

“The first one.”

“No. Why?”

“I’m looking at the restrictions.”

“Get the model running on ordinary machines first.”

“And if it works?”

“Then we’ll have something specific to ask for.”

Michael leaned back. He had wanted a larger answer. He had wanted the obstacles to be commensurate with what he thought the work could become.

“Can you send me the minimum setup?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Tonight, if you’ve already got it.”

A short silence.

“All right,” David said. “Let me finish dinner.”

Michael heard a plate being moved. He looked at his own, still beside the keyboard.

“Yeah. Tomorrow’s fine.”

He ended the call and went back to the supplier’s email. He asked which components were controlled, whether those restrictions applied to the destination or the end user, and what a reduced configuration would cost.

Before sending it, he removed the paragraph about the importance of the project.

The attachment was called export-information.xlsx.

He downloaded it.

03 · ChatGPT / Codex
We can develop both threads around a shared question: **can something retain its identity while appearing differently through different relationships?** That is a useful philosophical connection; each thread still needs its own evidence and definitions.

**First, your informiton proposal.** I’ll use *informiton* consistently for your proposed elementary unit of information.

The strongest starting point is to distinguish an underlying entity from its appearances. Let \(u\) be a hypothetical fundamental unit, and let different observational maps describe it:

\[
P_{\mathrm{visible}}(u)=0,
\qquad
P_{\mathrm{gravitational}}(u)\neq 0.
\]

This expresses “nothing from one angle, something from another”: the entity has no signal under one specified observation but does under another. It does **not** mean the entity both exists and does not exist in the same sense.

That distinction also clarifies the null set. In ordinary mathematics,

\[
\varnothing\neq\{\varnothing\}.
\]

The empty set contains no elements; the set containing the empty set contains one. An empty object can occupy a meaningful position within a larger structure. But a literal empty set cannot contain an information particle without ceasing to be empty. Your proposal therefore works better with **a state that appears empty under a particular observation** than with literal set-theoretic emptiness.

We can introduce your information requirement as an axiom:

\[
\mathcal I(u)\geq I_*>0.
\]

Here \(I_*\) is the proposed smallest information quantity. To make that substantive, we must define \(\mathcal I\): distinguishable states, communication capacity, entropy, and algorithmic complexity measure different things. A two-state system has a capacity of one bit; that does not imply every definite state has one bit of entropy. “Irreducible” also needs a rule specifying which decompositions are allowed.

Your three-pronged connection can then be represented as a relation among three sectors:

\[
\mathcal E(u)=\{L,D,G\},
\]

where \(L\) denotes the luminous or electromagnetically interacting sector, \(D\) the dark sector, and \(G\) gravitational geometry. This is a mathematical sketch of an **informiton connecting three domains**, rather than an established particle identification.

There is a crucial physical distinction: the axion is a hypothetical spin-zero particle and possible dark-matter constituent; a graviton, in the usual quantum description of gravity, has spin two. They cannot simply be the same ordinary particle with the same physical properties. The axion’s established theoretical motivation concerns the strong-CP problem, rather than mathematical axioms or emptiness. [Particle Data Group review](https://pdgweb.lbl.gov/2025/reviews/rpp2025-rev-axions.pdf)

A more coherent speculative claim is therefore:

> **The informiton is a proposed underlying information-bearing structure whose different collective manifestations might include axion-like matter and gravitational excitations.**

That preserves your unifying intuition while leaving room to explain their different properties. “Axiomatic” would describe the informiton’s role in your theory; “axion” would describe a particular physical manifestation. The similarity between the words is suggestive language, not evidence.

To advance this into physics, the next step would be to specify dynamics and derive a measurable prediction. A singularity would need a separate definition too: it cannot enter the identification merely because it evokes a point or an origin.

**Second, Muhammad, peace be upon him, as restoring Moses to the understanding of Christ.**

Your thesis has a defensible theological form:

> **Muhammad’s proclamation can be read as reaffirming Jesus as Messiah while placing him firmly within the prophetic continuity of Moses, revelation, law, and worship of the one God.**

The Qur’an explicitly presents Jesus as confirming the Torah and describes the Gospel as guidance and light. That supports your emphasis on continuity between Moses and Jesus. [Qur’an 5:46](https://quran.com/5/46)

It also retains the designation *Messiah* for Jesus while rejecting divine sonship and Trinitarian language. Consequently, “did not take Christ out” works if *Christ* means *Messiah*. It requires qualification if *Christ* includes the divine identity affirmed by mainstream Christian theology: that is a substantial disagreement about who Christ is. [Qur’an 4:171](https://quran.com/4/171)

“Reinserted Moses” likewise works best as an interpretive metaphor. Moses was already present in Christian scripture: Matthew portrays Jesus as affirming the Law and the Prophets. Christianity’s own account of fulfillment therefore needs to be part of the discussion. [Matthew 5:17–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%205%3A17-20&version=NRSVUE)

A sharper formulation of your argument would be:

> **In an Islamic reading, Muhammad reasserts the Mosaic context of Jesus’s mission: the Messiah belongs within the continuing revelation of the one God, with divine instruction shaping communal life.**

This need not mean restoring every Mosaic legal provision. The Qur’an both affirms preceding revelation and speaks of different communities receiving a law and a way. Continuity of divine source does not require identical legislation. [Qur’an 5:48](https://quran.com/5/48)

The productive connection between your threads is **unity with differentiated manifestations**. In the physics proposal, you posit one underlying structure with distinct observable expressions. In the theological proposal, you interpret distinct prophetic missions through continuity of divine source. That analogy can guide inquiry, while mathematical consistency and physical observation assess the first thread, and scripture, history, and theological argument assess the second.

> **We seek a coherent account of reality in which empirical investigation and faithfully examined revelation can inform one another, while every physical claim remains accountable to evidence and every theological interpretation remains accountable to its sources.**

That lets us pursue both threads together without making the success of your faith commitments depend on whether one speculative particle model survives.

There was room for several months of work between the two sides of that dash. He had given them roughly half an inch.

He scrolled to the calculation David had questioned. David had been irritatingly careful in the meeting, asking what each symbol meant while Michael was trying to explain where the whole thing might lead. Now, alone, he found that he couldn’t answer one of the questions.

He reread the paragraph. Then he read it again, moving his lips.

He had used the same symbol for a state and a measurement of the state.

“Jesus Christ.”

He corrected the notation. Two lines later, an equality no longer followed.

For a while he sat with his hands between his knees. The refrigerator started up in the next room.

He wanted David to be wrong. He wanted that more immediately than he wanted the theory to be right, and he was tired enough to admit it. David already had a salary for this. David could discover a problem on Tuesday and return to it on Wednesday.

Michael opened an email.

*Found a notation problem in the second derivation. Might affect the result. I’m checking it.*

He nearly added an explanation of how the error had happened. Instead he sent the three sentences.

Then he went back to the spreadsheet and put a comment in F38: *Unfunded. Previous figure removed.*

The application was now too small to support the work it described.

He took the plate to the kitchen. Ate two mouthfuls standing up. Put the rest in the fridge.

When he returned, a reply from the programme officer had arrived. Before they could discuss support, they needed the names and national affiliations of everyone expected to access the proposed computing environment.

He began a list. One collaborator was between institutions. Another used equipment owned by a university in a country that might complicate the application. Michael knew what would happen if he put an asterisk beside the name: a call, a request for clarification, another week.

He put the asterisk in.

Then he opened the officer’s attachment to find out exactly what it would cost them.
