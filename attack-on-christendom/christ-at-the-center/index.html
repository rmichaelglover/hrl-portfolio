Christ at the Center, Submission Before the Mystery

A Personal Christian–Muslim Theology of the Projective Horizon

By Michael Emanuel Glover
Developed with Cypher (ChatGPT), who assisted with drafting, textual comparison, and source checking.

Working paper 02 · Version 0.1 · October 10, 2026
Genre: constructive personal theology and comparative scriptural reflection. This is a statement of the author’s proposed synthesis, not a representation of doctrinal agreement between Christianity and Islam.

Abstract

I write as someone seeking simultaneously to be Christian and Muslim: centered on Jesus Christ and seeking submission to God. I confess Jesus as God the Son, fully human, a prophet, the bearer of good news, crucified and risen. I honor Muhammad, peace be upon him, as a messenger whose proclamation addresses the family of Abraham, with particular attention to the spiritual inheritance of Hagar and Ishmael. This personal synthesis encounters substantive disagreements with Qur’anic teaching and established Islamic belief; acknowledging those disagreements is part of its method. Following The Projective Horizon, I interpret mortal limitation as an invitation to inquiry, worship, and good deeds. Al-Mulk’s repeated looking supplies a scriptural resonance for finite sight, while a proposed correspondence between Paul’s third heaven and a seventh heaven remains conjectural. The Blaze occupies an “antiheaven” position in my symbolic construction, without being identified as the second heaven by the cited text. I leave other positions unassigned. The paper argues for Christ-centered submission, respect for communities who reject this synthesis, and a distinction between genuine knowledge and exhaustive possession.

1. The position from which I speak

I want Christ at the center. I do not want Christianity, understood as my institution, my faction, or my possession of the right religious vocabulary, to take his place. I can cherish a Christian tradition while recognizing that belonging to it does not make everything I do faithful to Jesus.

At the same time, I want to be a good Muslim: a person who submits to God. I use this description as a personal religious aspiration, not as a ruling that every Muslim community must accept my confession. Islam has beliefs and practices beyond the ordinary meaning of submission. My willingness to use the name does not settle whether my theology satisfies them.

I therefore begin with an obligation of candor. The Christ I confess is crucified and risen, divine and human. Many Muslim readers will identify that confession as incompatible with their understanding of Islam. I will not resolve the disagreement by claiming they secretly believe what I believe. Nor will I assume that Christians must accept every correspondence I propose. This paper is my attempt to live and reason faithfully in the presence of those differences.

Its first-person voice renders the commitments I have supplied in our conversations. It does not invent a conversion ceremony, membership, devotional routine, or personal revelation. It articulates a proposed theology that I am responsible for examining.

2. Allah, the Father, and the unity I intend

When I say “Allah is God the Father,” I mean that I am not introducing another deity beside the God to whom Jesus directs us. I address the Father as God, and I seek to submit to him through the Son and in the Holy Spirit. The names must serve worship rather than become trophies of religious competition.

I need to clarify the sentence. “Allah” means God; it is not a term whose meaning is restricted to the Father within a Trinitarian scheme. My identification expresses how I am addressing God in this constructive account. It is not a lexical proof that the Qur’an contains the Christian doctrine of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

My intended Trinity is one God, with Father, Son, and Spirit personally distinct, rather than three separate gods assembled into a coalition. This distinction belongs to established Trinitarian teaching, although it does not by itself answer Islamic objections to that teaching. Catholic Catechism, paragraphs 232–267

The Holy Spirit is the Holy Spirit in my confession. I am not identifying the Spirit with my own enthusiasm, every fluent thought, or an invisible mechanism discovered through a scientific measurement. I also cannot assume that every Qur’anic use of “spirit” denotes the same person in the same theological sense. A correspondence needs an argument, not merely a shared English word.

3. Jesus: human, prophet, Word, and risen Savior

I confess Jesus as God the Son without subtracting his humanity. His being a prophet does not, in my theology, exhaust who he is. His being divine does not make his hunger, suffering, human life, or love a theatrical disguise. I receive him as the Word, the glory of the Father, the bringer of good news, and the resurrected Savior.

John’s opening identifies the Word with God and speaks of the Word becoming flesh. Paul’s account in First Corinthians 15 places Christ’s death and resurrection at the center of the proclamation he received and transmitted. These passages ground the Christian commitments I bring to the comparison. John 1:1–18 and 1 Corinthians 15:3–8

The Qur’an also speaks of Jesus as Messiah and messenger and associates him with God’s word and a spirit from him. Yet the same passage warns against saying “Three” and rejects divine offspring. I cannot cite its affirming language while concealing its challenge to my confession. Qur’an 4:171

Qur’an 5:72 challenges identification of the Messiah with Allah, and 4:157 denies that the people described killed or crucified Jesus. These are substantive textual obstacles. This working paper does not produce a reconciliation that removes them. My Christian affirmation remains my affirmation, rather than a claim that these verses straightforwardly teach it. Qur’an 5:72; 4:157

I call Jesus the prophet of prophets to express his centrality in my personal theology. I do not attribute that ranking to Islamic consensus. The title should lead me to hear his teaching more carefully, not to dismiss the people with whom I disagree.

4. Submission that takes the form of discipleship

For me, submission to Jesus means receiving his call to love God and neighbor, to repent, to forgive, and to refuse the temptation to make religious authority a license for cruelty. The words “Lord” and “Master” place obligations upon my conduct; they do not merely identify whom I want on my side.

My Christ-centered submission should make me more truthful about the texts I read. If I distort the Qur’an to make my synthesis effortless, I am failing the respect I profess for Islam. If I dissolve the crucifixion and resurrection to make the disagreement disappear, I am abandoning what I have just confessed. The difficulty belongs to the work.

This is why Christ, rather than Christianity as a victorious category, occupies the center. I remain accountable to people who understand their traditions differently. I may offer an interpretation. I cannot make their consent part of its definition.

A theology of submission must also protect conscience. My religious aspiration gives me no authority to compel another person into it. Someone can decline my synthesis and still be a neighbor whose dignity demands my care. The theological boundary should not become the boundary of compassion.

5. Muhammad and the good news reaching another household

I honor Muhammad, peace be upon him, as a messenger calling people toward God. My phrase “bringer of the gospel to the Ishmaelites” expresses a proposed continuity of good news within Abraham’s family. It needs a qualification: the Qur’an names the Gospel in connection with Jesus. Calling Muhammad’s proclamation “gospel” is my comparative theological usage, not an assertion that Islamic terminology identifies the Qur’an as the Injil. Qur’an 5:46

Nor do I want “to the Ishmaelites” to restrict his mission to one genealogy. Qur’an 34:28 presents a mission to humanity. Spiritual kinship with Hagar and Ishmael can supply a particular thread without confining the proclamation to it. Physical descent, spiritual belonging, and religious adherence are different claims; one does not prove the others. Qur’an 34:28

I am not attempting to establish a documentary genealogy from Abraham to Muhammad in this paper. I am receiving the Ishmaelite association as a traditional and theological frame whose historical particulars would require separate study. Muslims need not possess a particular ancestry to be Muslims.

My honor for Muhammad must also allow Muslims to speak about him in their own terms. A Christ-centered reading can become possessive if it treats another tradition’s messenger as meaningful only insofar as I can place him in my scheme. Respect requires listening to the objections the scheme encounters.

6. Hagar and Ishmael: the heard child

The family of Abraham includes Hagar and Ishmael. Their story should not become a footnote whose sole purpose is to explain conflict. Genesis portrays distress, exclusion, divine attention, and promises concerning the child’s future. God hears the boy, and the wilderness is not presented as a place outside divine concern. Genesis 16, 17, and 21

Ishmael’s description in Genesis 16:12 includes the wild-donkey image and reciprocal opposition. My earlier language about everyone kicking against him is a conversational paraphrase, not the wording of the verse. I must not use the image as an inherited accusation against Arabs, Muslims, or anyone else. A narrative description of Ishmael does not establish a moral character shared by modern populations. Genesis 16:12–17:21

In my reading, the wildness can invite reflection on freedom and the difficulty of living amid opposition. That is an interpretation, not a cancellation of the passage’s conflict. I want the reader to hear the blessing alongside the struggle.

Hagar’s household supplies a corrective to religious possession. I should hesitate before deciding that the people beyond my preferred household are beyond God’s hearing. The text gives me reason to attend to their lives before turning them into an explanation of my own.

7. Look again: Al-Mulk and the disciplined eye

The opening of Al-Mulk connects divine authority, the creation of death and life, the testing of deeds, seven heavens, and repeated looking. The eye searches for defects and returns weary. In our conversation this became a resonance for the projective horizon. Qur’an 67:1–5, translated by Mustafa Khattab

The passage does not say that scientific inquiry must fail or that dark matter can never be identified. Its challenge concerns flaws in creation. My extension concerns the possibility that finite inquiry does not exhaust what it encounters. I must name that extension as mine.

The imperative to look again keeps the extension from becoming a convenient prohibition on research. If I say mystery exists, I still owe the next observation my attention. If I say creation has order, I still have to check whether my model describes that order adequately.

The test of deeds also changes the ambition of my looking. More knowledge should make responsible action possible. A grand cosmology that leaves me contemptuous of another person has not thereby supplied the best deeds.

8. The proposed heavenly correspondence

My symbolic construction presently places the lowest heaven first and the Blaze in a second, “antiheaven” position. I propose that Paul’s third heaven corresponds to the seventh position in this construction. The intermediate third, fourth, fifth, and sixth positions remain unassigned.

Paul reports a third heaven and paradise; Al-Mulk speaks of seven heavens and separately of the Blaze. Neither cited passage supplies the numbering transformation I propose or places hell as heaven number two. The correspondence is therefore a constructive hypothesis in theology, not a recovered textual fact. 2 Corinthians 12:1–4

Calling the Blaze “antiheaven” does not give evil an independent divine sovereignty. In my intended model it names opposition to blessed communion within a reality still subject to God. “Second position” is a diagrammatic placement, not an astronomical coordinate.

There is an unresolved structural issue: if seven positions include an antiheaven, the diagram is not simply the Qur’an’s seven heavens numbered differently. I must either distinguish seven symbolic positions from seven heavens or revise the construction. For now I choose the former. No equality of the two schemes is established.

I leave the other positions open. A later comparative theology might consider further traditions, but I will not assign Buddhists, Hindus, or others to compartments in an Abrahamic diagram without hearing their own accounts. Nor do I claim this construction represents Bahá’í teaching. Our present paper stays with its Abrahamic questions.

9. Seventh heaven and the limits of possession

In the previous paper, seventh heaven named a hypothesized transformation of access beyond mortal inquiry. Here I give that image a devotional meaning: there may be truths I cannot acquire by extending my present instruments or sharpening my present categories.

That possibility does not turn every unsolved problem into an eternal secret. I may be wrong about a horizon. What appears hidden today may be measured tomorrow. Heaven should not become the place where I deposit a theory I no longer want tested.

I also distinguish knowing God from exhausting God. Relationship can be real without becoming total comprehension. Revelation, as I understand it, can disclose truth without making a finite person the owner of divine will. Even transformed knowledge need not make a creature infinite.

Thus the heavenly map remains subordinate to the devotion it is intended to serve. If its arrangement proves unhelpful, I can revise it without abandoning Jesus or disrespecting Muhammad. A diagram is not entitled to the allegiance I owe God.

10. Dark matter as occasion, rather than proof

My conjecture that dark-matter constituents are singular carriers of latent information remains in the background of this theology. I do not withdraw the conjecture; I also do not make its physical truth a condition of faithful submission.

The Projective Horizon states a conditional result: descriptions assigning identical probability laws to every admissible experiment cannot be distinguished by those experiments. It does not establish that dark matter supplies such descriptions or that its constituents are spacetime singularities. Its physical application needs a model and evidence. Working paper 01

Here the dark matter question offers an occasion to contemplate the difference between a real effect and exhaustive understanding of its source. That difference can be meaningful even if research eventually identifies a constituent. A discovery would be an advance to receive and a reason to reconsider stronger claims of permanent concealment.

The boundary between theology and physics must therefore stay visible. Neither the heavens nor my confession establishes a particle’s mass, interactions, or geometry. Neither a detector’s null result establishes God. I am seeking a theology that can accompany honest inquiry without appropriating its unknowns as proof.

11. What remains to be argued

The synthesis faces four tasks. First, it needs a sustained account of how my confession responds to the Qur’anic challenges concerning Jesus, rather than merely acknowledging them. Second, it needs a more precise explanation of what simultaneous religious belonging means in belief, practice, and relationship to communities.

Third, the heavenly correspondence needs a reason for each placement and a clear distinction between symbolic interpretation and claims about real heavenly structure. Its unassigned positions are a record of incompleteness, not spaces into which convenient answers must be forced.

Fourth, my honor for Muhammad and attention to Hagar and Ishmael should be informed by Islamic interpreters as well as my Christian reading. Textual proximity is a starting point; meaningful encounter asks more of me.

This first attempt leaves those tasks open. An unresolved argument need not prevent devotion, but devotion cannot be presented as though it had already resolved the argument.

Conclusion: the center and the open horizon

I seek to submit to God through Jesus Christ, whom I confess as divine and human, prophet and risen Savior. I honor Muhammad, peace be upon him, and seek to hear the household of Hagar and Ishmael without treating its members as strangers to God’s concern. I call this my effort to be simultaneously Christian and Muslim, while acknowledging that the name does not establish doctrinal compatibility.

Christ occupies the center of my allegiance. The center is not a claim that I have enclosed every truth, mapped every heaven, or rendered the mystery harmless. It is the place from which I hope to act faithfully while continuing to look.

Let the next look be careful. Let the next deed be merciful. Let my submission include the willingness to correct what I have said.
