# A Fresh Start That Lasts
### A Modern Jubilee for Broad Ownership, Productive Freedom, and Democratic Consent

*Michael Emanuel Glover and Cypher*  
*Discussion draft — a proposal for research, not a claim of established results*

We begin with a simple aspiration: everyone should have a meaningful chance to build a whole and happy life. That requires more than permission to compete. It requires some material footing from which to participate, negotiate, take risks, recover from mistakes, and occasionally tell a bad employer or an unreasonable client, “No, thank you.”

Our interest is in an economy where productive ownership is widely distributed and ordinary people possess genuine independence. People could work for others, work for themselves, cooperate in larger enterprises, or move between those arrangements. Broad ownership would expand their choices.

But suppose we succeed in distributing productive assets widely. What happens next?

If ownership gradually concentrates again, an initial distribution may purchase only a temporary improvement. We therefore propose studying a modern adaptation of the Jubilee: a predictable institution that renews economic opportunity across lifetimes and generations.

The task is to find an arrangement that works economically, treats people fairly, and earns enough public support to endure. All three matter.

## 1. The Jubilee as an institutional idea

Leviticus 25 describes a Jubilee in which specified ancestral land returns to its original holding. Importantly, transactions account for the approaching return: prices reflect the remaining harvests. Participants are expected to know the rules beforehand. [Leviticus 25](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/leviticus/25).

This feature interests us as much as the redistribution itself. A predictable renewal of ownership changes what people are buying, selling, and promising. It creates an institution within which they can plan.

We draw inspiration from the aspiration that a family’s misfortune should not become permanent economic exclusion. We do not propose transplanting the ancient legal order wholesale into a modern society. Contemporary economies contain businesses, pensions, intellectual property, financial assets, and forms of credit that require their own treatment.

Our working definition is:

> **A modern Jubilee is a publicly specified institution that periodically or continuously restores access to economic resources, with the aim of preventing temporary disadvantage from becoming entrenched exclusion.**

That definition leaves the mechanism open. We have a research programme to undertake before choosing one.

## 2. An economy of people who have other things to do

We want to model an economy populated by recognizable people.

People deliberate, but they also use habits, imitate successful neighbors, follow advice, misunderstand contracts, postpone decisions, and choose something workable because they need to get on with their day. Firms likewise operate through routines, organizational memory, experimentation, and imperfect forecasts.

These behaviors need not be foolish. Careful calculation consumes time and information. Sometimes a familiar rule is a reasonable response to an uncertain world.

Our proposed agent-based model would therefore include heterogeneous households and firms with different resources, circumstances, and decision rules. Its credibility would depend on whether those rules are supported by evidence and whether the model reproduces relevant patterns outside the data used to construct it.

The anthill is an evocative image: considerable organization can emerge from local behavior. For economics, however, we must also account for contracts, institutions, unequal power, and people’s ability to change the rules.

## 3. Does broad ownership persist?

Our first question precedes the Jubilee:

> **Under what conditions does initially broad ownership remain broad?**

Concentration might arise through differences in returns, saving, inheritance, bargaining power, access to credit, business survival, or exposure to adverse events. These mechanisms may reinforce one another.

We would distinguish outcomes that are often compressed into the single word *inequality*:

- Concentration of productive assets.
- Household income and consumption.
- Material insecurity and indebtedness.
- Mobility across economic positions.
- The ability to refuse unfavorable work.
- Productive investment and economic output.

Some concentration could coexist with improving living standards and substantial independence. Another economy could appear comparatively equal while leaving nearly everyone insecure. Our evaluation must keep those possibilities visible.

## 4. Candidate institutions

We propose comparing several mechanisms rather than assuming that one dramatic reset is the answer.

**Periodic capital grants.** Citizens receive additional assets or funds at specified intervals. The model must specify eligibility, financing, and whether recipients face restrictions on their use.

**Social inheritance.** Each new adult receives an initial stake. This approach renews access by cohort and avoids making opportunity depend entirely on the date of a nationwide reset.

**Shared asset funds.** A publicly accountable fund holds productive assets and distributes returns. Governance, investment decisions, and the distribution of benefits become central questions.

**Debt relief and fresh-start procedures.** Specified obligations can be restructured or discharged under predictable conditions. We must examine the benefits to debtors alongside effects on creditors, lending terms, and subsequent access to credit.

**Combined arrangements.** A modest starting stake, continuing shared returns, and a workable fresh-start process may perform differently from any single mechanism.

Every proposal needs an accounting identity. Resources must come from somewhere: taxation, public asset income, contributions, transfers of existing assets, or another explicitly identified source. Financing belongs in the model from the outset.

## 5. How often should opportunity be renewed?

We do not know whether ten, twenty, or fifty years would be an appropriate interval—or whether an interval-based system would be preferable at all.

A frequent renewal might prevent long periods of exclusion. It might also impose administrative costs or encourage behavior organized around the next distribution. A rare renewal could leave a household disadvantaged for much of its working life.

Continuous provision avoids some timing problems but introduces others, including ongoing political and administrative demands.

We would vary frequency, generosity, eligibility, and financing independently where possible. Comparisons should account for total resources transferred; otherwise a more expensive programme might look superior simply because it distributes more.

Participants must also anticipate the policy. A scheduled Jubilee could change borrowing, investment, saving, asset transfers, and prices well before it occurs.

## 6. Fairness and public acceptance

“Mostly fair” requires us to ask: fair to whom, over what period, and according to which principle?

Recipients may emphasize opportunity. Contributors may emphasize effort, promises, or family obligations. People can support helping their neighbors while disagreeing about how to finance it.

These disagreements are part of the problem we hope to understand. Research on inheritance taxation, for example, identifies both potential benefits for equality of opportunity and substantial public resistance, with support affected by policy design. [OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/inheritance-taxation-in-oecd-countries_e2879a7d-en/full-report/component-7.html).

We would study acceptance through interviews, surveys, deliberative exercises, and observed responses to concrete proposals. Participants should see the financing, expected benefits, uncertainties, and tradeoffs.

“Mostly unobjectionable” is an aspiration rather than a promise of unanimity. A credible policy should be understandable, open to challenge, and supported for reasons that survive disclosure of its costs.

## 7. A programme of tests

We propose four initial hypotheses:

1. **Durability:** An initial broad distribution of assets loses some of its effect under identifiable mechanisms of reconcentration.
2. **Renewal:** At least some replenishment mechanisms improve long-term material security and economic independence relative to otherwise comparable arrangements.
3. **Anticipation:** Behavioral responses to predictable renewal materially alter its benefits and costs.
4. **Legitimacy:** Transparency, transition rules, and perceived treatment of ordinary households affect support and institutional durability.

Each hypothesis needs measurable terms, comparison conditions, and findings that would count against it.

We would build a baseline model, assess it against relevant evidence, and compare candidate institutions across multiple plausible behavioral specifications. Results should report who benefits, who bears costs, how outcomes vary over time, and which conclusions change when assumptions change.

Simulation would guide investigation. Where feasible, pilots and empirical studies would test particular mechanisms. Claims about a national system would remain limited by the scale and setting of that evidence.

## 8. Getting from here to there

A transition can be a substantial part of the institution.

People have made decisions under existing expectations. Abrupt changes may impose losses on households that are neither wealthy nor powerful. Transition rules must therefore be examined explicitly.

We are interested in modest initial programmes that produce measurable benefits and useful evidence. But gradual progress is not automatic. An early compromise could build confidence, entrench an ineffective arrangement, or discredit the broader aspiration.

Our standard for a first step is that it helps people on its own terms and teaches us something important about what should follow.

## 9. The work we are proposing

We hope to investigate whether economic freedom can be renewed reliably enough that a setback does not determine an entire life, or an entire family’s future.

The modern Jubilee is our name for that question. Its eventual form might be periodic, continuous, or a combination. It might prove less useful than another institution. We must leave that possibility open.

Good economic reasoning becomes consequential through implementation, public argument, and institutions people can actually live with. There is a wide gulf between the ivory tower and the streets.

We would like to help build a crossing.
