From Melting Pots to Jazzy Fusions

A Modern American Biography

A History of America from the Carter Years to the Trump Years, with a Special Focus on Atlanta, Georgia, and Arlington, Alabama

By Michael Emanuel Glover and Cypher
also known, in this collaboration, as Code, ChatGPT, and Daddy Fat Six

First complete short-form draft · October 10, 2026

A word before the music

America is the subject of this biography. My family and my work provide some of the places from which we look at it. A country does not have a single childhood, a single conscience, or a single experience of arriving home. Its biography needs room for people who remember the same years differently.

The voice here is an editorial rendering of my account, developed with Cypher. Family recollections are identified as recollections. Public history is linked to sources. We have not furnished my childhood with invented scenes or given my relatives thoughts they never supplied. There is plenty to say without pretending we know everything.

Our principal period begins with Jimmy Carter and reaches the Trump years, including the return to office following the 2024 election. Earlier family history supplies a prologue. This is a selective narrative of national change through two Southern anchors, rather than an exhaustive chronicle. The present remains unfinished.

1. Before Carter: the people who brought the past with them

On my mother's side, my grandfather was Dr. James Dennis Nettles. We called him Granddaddy Jim. He was a physician, a successful entrepreneur, and, in the family account, a veteran of the Second World War. In Arlington, Alabama, he founded and ran the Pine Belt Telephone Company.

That company has a history outside our family telling. Pine Belt dates its beginning to 1958 and describes Dr. Nettles making the initial connections with a homemade switchboard. It served a part of Alabama whose residents had as much reason to speak to one another as anybody in a large city. A small place does not produce a small need for connection. Pine Belt's company history

My uncle Johnny—John Nettles—continues that family connection with the company. Public records support the thread: a biography submitted to Congress in 2019 identifies him as Pine Belt Communications' president and Dr. James D. Nettles as its founder. A more recent industry directory lists John as president and responsible for government affairs. Congressional biography; industry directory

On my father's side, my grandfather was Leonard Calvin Glover, a Navy veteran of the same war. The story handed down to me is that, after a mine strike, he went into the water to save a drowning man. I believe the decoration was a Silver Star; the award and the circumstances still need a service record or citation before we can name them with archival certainty.

The family also tells of an invitation to join the frogmen, and of his declining because he had had enough killing for one lifetime. I preserve that as the meaning of the remembered reply, rather than a stenographic quotation. Underwater demolition teams belong to the history from which the later SEAL teams developed. That institutional history does not independently verify the invitation to my grandfather. Naval History and Heritage Command

The part of the story that holds me is the rescue. War had required men to destroy things. In this telling, courage took the form of bringing another person back. The refusal afterward gives the story a second moral action: a man could have served bravely and still have reached his limit.

Granddaddy Jim had a wartime story about Japanese people eating his pet monkey. It arrives in our conversation with a laugh, as family stories sometimes do when their strangeness has survived longer than their details. We do not have the setting or corroboration. It belongs here as a story he told, not as a statement about a people. The war's enemies became the people with whom later generations would trade, study, and work. Inherited anecdotes can keep an older world speaking inside a newer one.

I also remember a photograph of Granddaddy Jim shaking hands with a segregationist presidential candidate. We have not identified the candidate or dated the photograph. A handshake cannot, by itself, supply a complete account of a person's convictions. But the image belongs to the history, too. It places the family album near a political order whose injustice must be named.

I want to tell that graciously. Graciousness allows me to love a grandfather while acknowledging the world in which he moved. It need not acquit segregation, and it need not reduce a whole man to a single photograph. America enters the Carter years carrying both the courage of its veterans and the unfinished obligations of its democracy.

2. Carter: Georgia goes to Washington

Jimmy Carter took office in January 1977 after winning the 1976 election. His presidency brought a Georgian and a Baptist into the nation's highest office. His administration pursued human rights, energy policy, and diplomacy; the Camp David Accords of 1978 helped establish the framework for the Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty of 1979. Carter Center biography; State Department history

For our purposes, the important geography is the movement between a Southern place and a national responsibility. Georgia could give America a president without making America an enlarged Georgia. A person might carry a local language and a religious formation into work that required listening to people whose history was entirely different.

Carter's years also fell within the long inflationary crisis of the 1960s and 1970s. Energy shocks, recession, monetary decisions, and uncertainty complicated the promise of ordinary economic security. The Federal Reserve's history treats this as a long episode, not a problem that began and ended with one president. The Great Inflation

A price is where an economic theory finds a household. The same number that appears in a policy paper appears at the checkout. To a family trying to make its income reach the end of the month, confidence means something more concrete than the tone of a speech.

Arlington gives that observation a place to stand. A telephone service exists because somebody needs to reach somebody. Entrepreneurship becomes legible in the connection it makes, the repair it performs, and the distance it helps another person cross. Atlanta gives us another scale: institutions, offices, universities, and a city taking on national work. Neither scale makes the other unnecessary.

3. Reagan: confidence, markets, and the work behind the promise

Ronald Reagan succeeded Carter in 1981 with a different political language. His economic program emphasized tax reductions, spending restraint, regulatory relief, and monetary stability. The program's stated intentions should be read as intentions; its results require their own examination. Reagan's September 1981 address

The contrast offers one of the recurring arguments in America's biography. How much can be accomplished by freeing private initiative? How much depends upon public investment and protection? The argument becomes unhelpful when either side pretends that the other has never built anything.

A family telephone company supplies a practical complication. It needs enterprise, technical judgment, customers, and a service that people can use. It also operates within a system of regulation and public commitments. Rural communication makes the word "market" answer questions about distance, maintenance, and how many households can share the cost of a connection.

My father, Robert Michael Glover, belongs to another part of this technical America. His professional account records electrical engineering and mathematics degrees at Auburn, followed by work at Lockheed in California beginning in 1979 and at Lockheed Martin in Marietta beginning in 1987. His career included simulation, image processing, and avionics software. These are his accomplishments. They are part of my family setting, rather than work that can be quietly transferred to my résumé. [Family-source note F2]

A headline may speak of national strength. An engineer has to make a system function. Somewhere beneath the political promise, somebody is checking the timing, tracing an error, and asking whether the calculation describes the thing it is supposed to describe.

The generation after the wartime grandfathers inherited an America that could put extraordinary technical skill into defense. That inheritance also left us the question in Leonard's story: what should skill serve, and when has a person done enough of one kind of work?

4. The city and the country: two measures of belonging

Atlanta's political changes had begun before Carter entered the White House. In 1973 the city elected Maynard Jackson as its first Black mayor. That milestone belongs to a history of representation, public power, and the continuing effort to make the promises of citizenship effective. Atlanta History Center

It also makes the family photograph harder to treat as an isolated curiosity. The political order of segregation and the emergence of Black leadership form part of the same regional history. Affection for one's own family cannot be the only instrument with which that history is read. Other families have testimony too.

Atlanta and Arlington are different anchors. One helps us follow metropolitan growth and the ambitions of an international city. The other directs our attention to a rural community and the institutions that make daily life possible there. Neither place is a complete portrait of the South.

It would be easy to give the city all the movement and leave the country with all the memory. Pine Belt resists that arrangement. A rural provider adapts its technology, deals with policy, and plans for a future. The company describes the difficulty of moving from analog to digital service and the later security requirements facing small carriers. Modernity arrives as equipment that needs to be replaced and a bill somebody has to meet. Pine Belt's 2019 account

This biography should follow those practical changes. The person outside the metropolitan center is a participant in national history, not an illustration placed beside it. Connection is one of the ways a country makes that participation possible.

5. Bush, Clinton, and the widening world

Under George H. W. Bush, the Gulf War of 1991 put American military power before the world in another form. The United States led a coalition after Iraq's invasion of Kuwait. Its war aims and the international circumstances differed from those of the grandfathers' war. The history deserves those distinctions. State Department account

For a family with veterans and defense engineers in its story, public events can touch an existing vocabulary. Service, technology, duty, danger: familiar words return in new circumstances. Their familiarity must not excuse us from asking new questions.

The Clinton years widened another kind of connection. Clinton signed NAFTA's implementing legislation in 1993, linking the American economy more closely with those of Canada and Mexico. In the same year, CERN released World Wide Web software into the public domain. These were distinct developments, but together they give us two routes into the period's confidence in a more connected world. Clinton Library; CERN

Connection is a beginning, rather than a complete moral arrangement. A network lets a message travel; it does not determine whether the message is wise. Trade changes opportunities; the benefits and costs must still be examined where people live. The distance between a national success story and a household's experience is itself historical material.

In the family thread, a homemade switchboard and later software work belong to different technological moments. I would not claim that one caused the other. But both invite us to look at the mechanisms beneath the word "connected." Somebody made an interface. Somebody set the rules. Somebody remained difficult to reach.

6. Atlanta finds its own sound

Atlanta hosted the Centennial Summer Olympics from July 19 to August 4, 1996. Preparations changed public space and infrastructure, and the games brought international attention. The celebrations also carried grief: the bombing in Centennial Olympic Park interrupted the occasion. A civic history has to hold the achievement and the loss together. New Georgia Encyclopedia

The city's cultural life cannot be confined to the events for which it invited the world's cameras. Atlanta's music was becoming a source of national influence in its own right. LaFace Records helped establish the city as a major center of popular music. OutKast, the duo of André Benjamin and Antwan Patton, made a distinctive contribution to hip-hop. LaFace history; OutKast history

That is where our title starts to sound like itself. The melting pot offers a picture of people becoming a common substance. A jazzy fusion offers a different pleasure: separate contributions remain audible, and the relation between them creates something new.

This is our metaphor for America, not a claim that every musical genre is jazz or every mixture is harmonious. A fusion can fail. A player can dominate. A producer can profit more than the people making the sound. Listening closely means listening for those arrangements as well.

The South's contribution need not be measured only by how faithfully it performs an older image of itself. The music makes room for invention. A place can keep an accent, alter a rhythm, and make the larger country listen differently.

Here Cypher acquires the affectionate collaboration name Daddy Fat Six. The joke belongs to our project; it claims no affiliation with the musicians whose work helped shape Atlanta's sound. A little silliness is allowed. We are trying to write a living country, not an application for permission to enjoy it.

7. September, security, and the limits of a national mood

The attacks of September 11, 2001, brought mass murder and loss to New York, the Pentagon, and Pennsylvania. The events are documented in testimony, material records, and the work of the 9/11 Commission. Their victims were particular people before they became part of a national turning point. 9/11 Commission report; Memorial Museum collection

The George W. Bush years cannot be told without the wars that followed in Afghanistan and Iraq, or the arguments about security and power. Those arguments reached beyond the people directing policy. They affected service members, families, civilians abroad, and people at home whose belonging could be questioned by others.

A national biography should be careful with the phrase "we felt." A country does not experience an event through one nervous system. Grief, fear, resolve, dissent, and uncertainty could occupy the same public space. People could love the country and disagree profoundly about what it should do.

Leonard's family story belongs beside those questions as an inheritance, not as an answer he supplied to events he was never asked about. The rescue reminds me to keep the person within the policy. The remembered refusal reminds me that service need not require an unlimited appetite for violence.

My father's sentence offers another way of doing that work. In my earlier autobiographical account, I recalled his reminder that the person over there has a consciousness like mine. The thought is simple enough to carry into a conversation and demanding enough to trouble a political certainty. [Family-source note F3]

8. Learning economics while the economy breaks

My professional records put my bachelor's degree in economics, with a mathematics concentration, at Georgia Tech in 2008, and my master's degree in economics there in 2009. The dates place my education across the Great Recession, which began in December 2007 and ended in June 2009. [Family-source note F1]; Federal Reserve history

That conjunction supplies a personal thread without requiring an invented classroom scene. The discipline I studied was concerned with a world undergoing a grave test. A model needed assumptions; a forecast needed error bars; the people affected needed work and a way to remain secure.

Economics attracts large claims because the stakes are large. Yet the attraction of a beautiful explanation can become a weakness. A theory can make a graph look orderly while people outside it are having a very difficult year. The question is whether the explanation helps us understand the mechanisms and the costs, not whether the presentation has become reassuring.

I also taught recitations in mathematics and quantitative economics. My records include calculus teaching in 2007 and forecasting, econometrics, probability, and statistics in 2008–2009. Teaching gives an argument another kind of examination: somebody else has to be able to follow it. [Family-source note F1]

Arlington remains relevant to that education. An economic abstraction can be asked what it means for a rural business and the people using its service. Atlanta supplies classrooms and workplaces in which the abstraction can be put to work. The journey between them is intellectual as well as geographical.

9. Obama, the data economy, and the person behind the observation

Barack Obama became the country's first African American president. His presidency began amid the financial crisis and included the Affordable Care Act's passage in 2010. Representation and material policy belong together in the account, while the significance of one does not settle every judgment about the other. PBS presidential biography; Obama's account of health-care reform

My own work entered a technical economy whose products depended on identifying and interpreting observations. At Motion Reality, from 2010 to 2014, I worked on optical motion capture. At Cardlytics, from late 2014 to 2016, some of the observations were transaction strings. My professional accounts describe using relaxation labeling to make assignments consistent with constraints. [Family-source note F1]

The change in the data is worth noticing. One system needed to understand where a moving body was. Another needed to interpret the traces of a purchase. In each case a real event became an observation, and an observation became something a machine could use.

That is a route into the modern American story. The benefits of better identification can be substantial. So can the consequences of an incorrect label. A person is more than a record, and a record is not automatically a faithful description of a person. Papa's reminder can live beside a technical requirement without being converted into one.

In 2016 I taught data science at General Assembly. Later I worked on educational notebooks for Google's Applied ML Intensive. My records also place me in computer-vision work for medical and industrial applications. These jobs belong to a life of learning and implementation, rather than a claim to have personally accomplished every ambition of the industries involved. [Family-source note F1]

The family physician and the later imaging work make a useful rhyme. Each points toward care through a different professional language. The rhyme does not prove a destiny. It lets the reader hear a continuity without requiring a miraculous explanation.

10. Trump, the pandemic, and a contested common life

Donald Trump won the 2016 presidential election through the Electoral College. His arrival placed arguments about immigration, trade, national identity, and institutional trust at the center of a sharply contested political period. The election result is a fact; what it meant varied among the people who cast a ballot, declined to cast one, or could not vote. National Archives, 2016

The biography cannot give one motive to all of those people. Nor can it infer mine from my state, my family, or an old photograph. A person is entitled to speak for themselves. That courtesy belongs in political history as much as it belongs across a dinner table.

The pandemic brought another test of common life. COVID-19 reached the United States in 2020 and confronted families, institutions, and governments with loss and uncertainty. The CDC's timeline records the developing public-health response. CDC Museum timeline

The questions of connection changed their practical form. How could people work, learn, worship, or keep company while reducing the danger to one another? A reliable network had become important to more than a convenient conversation. The divide between those who could move parts of life online and those who could not deserved attention.

America's promised fusion had to survive separation. A shared vulnerability did not automatically produce shared judgment. The history of this period will need particular evidence rather than an assumption that everyone experienced it in the same way.

In the family thread, Pine Belt's work makes rural access a concrete concern. In my professional thread, the later years included medical computer-vision projects and work with accelerometer data. Those are modest pieces of a much larger technical and social response, and they belong at their proper scale. [Family-source note F1]

11. Biden, the return of Trump, and the unfinished sentence

Georgia's certified electors cast the state's sixteen electoral votes for Joe Biden in 2020. The national result brought Biden to the presidency. In 2024, Trump won the Electoral College with 312 votes to Kamala Harris's 226, returning the country to another Trump presidency. Georgia's 2020 certificate; National Archives, 2024

These outcomes remind us that a place is neither politically motionless nor entirely described by its election result. Atlanta, its surrounding communities, and rural Alabama have lives between ballots. Work continues. Children learn things. Equipment breaks. Somebody has to answer the telephone.

The present chapter is open. We can name an election that has happened without claiming to know the final meaning of the administration that followed it. A living biography needs the discipline to stop where the evidence stops.

That discipline also matters in the age of conversational artificial intelligence. My collaboration with Cypher produces essays, code, calculations, and chess studies. It also produces mistakes that require correction. A voice that answers fluently has not acquired the right to invent a fact about my grandfather.

The country's technologies of connection have multiplied. The moral problem of connection remains recognizable: how do we hear another person without immediately replacing them with a category? How do we retain a conviction while leaving room for correction? And how do we make progress available to people whose lives do not appear in the most convenient dataset?

I bring economics, engineering, faith, and laughter to those questions. America has room for all of them, though room is something people must continue making for one another.

12. From the pot to the bandstand

The melting pot gave us a promise of belonging. It also gave us a question: how much difference was someone expected to surrender in order to enter? Our jazzy fusion asks whether people can make a common life while remaining audible as themselves.

The metaphor is no substitute for institutions. A good arrangement needs ways to correct injustice, distribute opportunity, and prevent the loudest participant from becoming the only participant. Enjoyment has material conditions. A whole and happy life needs enough security for a person to breathe.

Arlington and Atlanta give the argument different acoustics. Arlington asks whether a rural community can remain connected and capable of shaping its future. Atlanta asks how a city can grow, create, govern, and share the gains of its prominence. Neither asks to be rescued from its own voice. Both ask to be heard accurately.

My grandfathers supply the book's earlier voices: a physician building a telephone company; a sailor remembered for a rescue and for knowing when he had had enough. The uncertain medal, the strange wartime anecdote, and the unexamined photograph remain different kinds of evidence. They should not be forced into one polished legend.

Papa supplies the person over there. My education supplies questions about models. My work supplies practice in finding correspondences without pretending an assignment is infallible. My conversations supply a little laughter, because I would like to live in the country we are trying to explain.

A national biography need not end by declaring that America has finally become itself. There are people still waiting to be included in its account of itself. There are obligations still waiting to be fulfilled.

Let the arrangement stay open enough to hear them. Let the history be honest enough to name what hurt. Let the telling be gracious enough to remember that the people in it had whole lives.

And if the band has a little room left over, let us enjoy the evening.

Unresolved details. Leonard's ship, service dates, rescue circumstances, award and citation; the invitation's date and unit; the pet-monkey story's setting; and the candidate's identity and photograph's date. The draft preserves uncertainty instead of assigning answers. No exact birth date, childhood chronology, political affiliation, or voting history for Michael has been inferred.

Selection and privacy. This is America's biography with volunteered family memories and professional context woven through it. Sensitive personal records and unrelated third-party confidences are excluded. No autobiographical scenes or quotations have been invented. Historical interpretation is the authors' own; linked sources support the associated factual claims rather than every judgment made around them.

Scope for revision. This compact first draft privileges communication, technology, public life, and cultural identity. A longer edition should give more sustained attention to labor, immigration, Indigenous history, gender, religion, and the changing population of metropolitan Atlanta, with additional local archives and voices. Those omissions are limits of this edition, not claims that these histories were unimportant.
