# The Christening of the American Christian Bolshevik Revolution

By Michael Emanuel Glover · Developed with Cypher (Codex) · October 10, 2026

*Fear God. Love the King. Enjoy England.*

The christening began after a chess game. One knight danced into sacrifice. Another survived an offer of its own destruction. A confident queen check met a modest pawn move, and the opponent resigned with nine minutes still available for reconsideration. The game was called “INCREDIBLE SULK: Mexizuko Dance Edition.” Sometimes the title arrives having already done the criticism.

Then came the mantra. Its first version was “Fear God. Love the King. Love England.” The last verb changed. England became something to enjoy. An American Christian Bolshevik revolution acquired, among its other responsibilities, the obligation to leave room for a picnic.

The joke carries the purpose. A political movement should make a whole and happy life possible for everyone. Bread matters. Shelter matters. Work should leave a person with enough of themselves to love someone, learn something, and sit down without being afraid of tomorrow. The enjoyment of a country belongs to its people, including those who cannot afford to buy much of it.

## Fear God

The first command places an obligation above political allegiance. In the Christian imagination of this movement, God judges the ruler as well as the ruled. A government cannot make its own pride sacred merely by announcing that it governs in God's name.

Fear here asks for humility before that judgment: repentance, an examination of one's conduct, and the recognition that power does not turn a person into the source of goodness. Those who speak most confidently about God's earthly representatives still have to answer for how they treat their neighbours.

Christianity already names the movement's allegiance to Christ. The mantra leaves “the king” unnamed because its earthly application is a question to be considered, rather than a permanent identification to be printed on every flag.

## Love the King

Love the king. Do not fear the king as you fear God.

An earthly ruler may deserve affection, gratitude, and cooperation. A democratically elected president may be such a ruler. A governor may retain a claim on public trust when a president abuses it. A religious authority may guide conscience when the state has become corrupt. These possibilities concern recognition and allegiance; the offices retain their different responsibilities and powers.

The question is who deserves that recognition here and now. Authority carries obligations. Its legitimacy must survive an examination of its conduct. Respect for an office cannot require the surrender of conscience to the person occupying it.

Love also leaves room for disagreement. One can wish a ruler well, pray for them, and peacefully refuse an unjust command. Civil disobedience need not begin with hatred. It can begin with the conviction that both the ruler and the neighbour harmed by the command deserve better.

Nor does naming a new authority settle every difficulty. Recognition needs reasons that other people can examine. Otherwise, the search for a worthy king becomes a succession of people willing to agree with us. The Christian demand for repentance applies to the person choosing an allegiance too.

## Enjoy England

England is the affectionate flourish. In this American formulation, it stands for the invitation to love and enjoy one's country: its people, its ordinary places, its peculiarities, and the life one can share there.

Enjoyment gives patriotism a human scale. A country is present in a walk, a sandwich, a game, a conversation, and a neighbour who has time to stop. Its worth cannot be exhausted by the dignity of its government or the expense of its ceremonies.

“Enjoy” also puts a demand upon the revolution. If everyone is to have a whole and happy life, the institutions we build must make enjoyment possible. A movement that consumes every hour, demands every allegiance, and postpones happiness until after the next victory has lost the purpose expressed in its own closing word.

There should be time for chess. There should be time to laugh at the chess. There should be room for a person to belong without having to march all afternoon.

## The baptism

The Bolshevism being christened here expresses the aspiration toward shared provision, solidarity, and a whole life for everyone. The word carries a historical burden, including authoritarianism and repression. This proposal takes responsibility for giving its own meaning plainly: a peaceful, democratic political imagination transformed by Christian commitments.

The transformation reaches the means. Mercy governs the treatment of opponents. Conscience protects the person who refuses to join. Democratic accountability makes the leadership answerable to those it claims to serve. Material care reaches people without requiring a declaration of religious allegiance in return.

The speaker's insistence is that Christianity already tames the beast. That is the faith being expressed in the name: love of neighbour changes what the movement is permitted to do. It cannot make the enemy disposable. It cannot demand cruelty as an entrance fee to a future happiness. Its protection must reach the dissenter too.

The final clarification matters: “baptized Christianity, not pagan Christendom.” Here baptism names a lived commitment to repentance and love. Christendom names the temptation to make earthly power sacred and call the resulting obedience Christian. The journal's attack is directed at that temptation. Political ambition must itself undergo the examination it would impose on others.

A christening gives a name and declares a commitment. The life that follows has to make the commitment visible. A hungry person should be fed. A neighbour should be heard. A proud ruler should be capable of correction. An opponent should remain a person.

Fear God. Love the King. Enjoy England.

Let it be written. Let it be done. And let there be enough life left over to enjoy the country whose happiness the revolution proposes to serve.
