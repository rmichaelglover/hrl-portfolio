Love First, Categorize Second

Attack on Christendom · Faith & Neighbourhood · October 9, 2026

An appeal to evangelical Christians concerning transgender people, Christian kinship, and the names by which we call one another.

Before you decide whether to say brother, sister, or sibling, remember that you have been called to love the person you are addressing. The noun matters. The commandment comes first.

This is a simple proposal, and a demanding one. A transgender person arrives at church, at your dinner table, or in your family. You may have questions about gender. You may have settled convictions. You may be unsure how to speak. None of these conditions gives you leave to suspend kindness until you have completed the classification.

The person is already there. Your opportunity to love is already there. Begin.

The order of our calling

Jesus gives his disciples a command to love one another and identifies that love as the public mark of discipleship. He does not describe expertise in sorting people as the mark by which the world will recognize his followers. This essay takes that order seriously: our treatment of another person is part of our Christian witness, not an incidental matter to be considered after the argument. John 13:34–35, KJV.

For a transgender Christian, the question is particularly immediate. Here is someone who professes faith, prays, and seeks fellowship. If you meet that person primarily as a controversy, you may fail to meet the fellow Christian standing in front of you. To speak of Christian family while withholding ordinary warmth is to make the language of kinship do very little work.

The duty also extends beyond the church. Your neighbour need not pass a membership examination to become eligible for mercy. In the parable of the Good Samaritan, the question of who qualifies as a neighbour gives way to the practical question of who acts as one. The person who helps does not wait for the injured man to supply an acceptable theological autobiography. That is the application I ask us to consider here. Luke 10:25–37, KJV.

Meet a person before debating an abstraction

The term “transgenderism” can make a conversation sound as though a system has entered the room. Usually, a person has. Speak about transgender people when you mean people. A broad label will not tell you what an individual believes, how that person understands faith, or what help is needed today.

Ask a question you can genuinely listen to: “What would you like me to call you?” “How have you been?” “Would you like to sit with us?” The answers belong to the person, not to the most alarming example somebody has circulated online.

We can admit that evangelical Christians disagree about the theology of gender without making every greeting carry the entire disagreement. This essay offers a pastoral argument about how to treat people. It does not claim that every evangelical church already accepts that argument, or that a single essay resolves every doctrinal question.

Names, pronouns, and Christian kinship

My recommendation is to use the name a person gives you and the pronouns that person asks you to use. When you are speaking directly to someone, the person's name and “you” will often suffice. When a form of Christian kinship is appropriate, ask whether brother, sister, or sibling is welcome. “Fellow Christian” can also express fellowship without requiring you to guess.

“Transgender” is ordinarily an adjective: a transgender person, a transgender woman, a transgender man. Avoid turning it into an impersonal collective, “the transgenders.” Ordinary speech should leave the person visible.

This recommendation is my ethical application of love, not a claim that the Bible contains a modern pronoun policy. Some Christians understand requested pronouns as ordinary courtesy; others believe they raise questions of conscience. Those questions deserve an honest conversation. The person you are addressing deserves an honest conversation too, rather than a public demonstration staged at that person's expense.

If conscience leaves you uncertain, begin with the person's name. Use it naturally. Seek a respectful way forward, and listen when the person explains how your language is received. Avoid making conspicuous linguistic manoeuvres into a substitute for relationship. Courtesy that is designed to feel like a rebuke has missed its own purpose.

If you make a mistake, correct it briefly and carry on. “Sorry—she. As I was saying…” will usually do more good than a speech about how difficult this has become for you. The meal, the prayer request, and the ordinary conversation deserve to continue.

Truth spoken by a loving tongue

The objection will come: “But Christians must tell the truth.” Yes. Truthfulness and love both make demands on us. A professed devotion to truth does not excuse ridicule, invasive questioning, gossip, or the deliberate use of language to wound.

James warns against praising God while cursing people made in God's likeness. Whatever conclusions you draw about gender, that warning applies to your own mouth. The person who speaks most confidently about another person's spiritual condition should still examine what the tongue is doing. James 3:9–10, KJV.

If a difficult theological conversation is wanted, have it with care, at an appropriate time, and with room for an answer. A greeting is not permission for an interrogation. A prayer request is not permission to publish someone's personal history. Friendship is not a waiting room in which you keep someone until your correction is ready.

Love takes an interest in the life actually being lived: whether someone has friends, whether work is going well, whether there is a place at the table. It can bring a meal, defend someone from mockery, keep a confidence, and offer company. These acts do not require mastery of every disputed term.

The family should sound like a family

We are called to love our brothers and sisters before we are called to settle whether a particular Christian should be addressed as brother, sister, or sibling. That is the thesis. It establishes a responsibility before a classification; it also asks that our eventual words serve that responsibility.

This is an attack on Christendom wherever religious respectability becomes permission to humiliate. Christianity supplies the appeal: love one another, practise mercy, and take responsibility for your speech. A church can hold a discussion about doctrine while still making room for a person to breathe.

Say hello. Learn the name. Listen to the answer. Offer the chair.

Let the first thing your neighbour learns about your Christianity be that love has already begun.
