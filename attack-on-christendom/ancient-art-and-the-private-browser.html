Before the Private Browser

A Tasteful Introduction to Ancient Pornography, with Apologies to the Committee for Respectable Amphorae.

Our ancient ancestors had many disadvantages: unreliable dentistry, difficult journeys, and no means of clearing their browser history. They solved the last problem by not having browsers. The images, however, survived.

The polite visitor may call them “studies of the human form.” The less polite visitor may ask whether the studies were conducted with both hands on the syllabus. Everybody understands the joke. Nobody needs a demonstration.

First, examine the label

“Ancient pornography” is a convenient modern heading, but a poor substitute for asking what an object was for. An erotic image might belong to household decoration, mythology, entertainment, or a protective symbol. Nudity alone does not establish either purpose or audience. A museum label begins an inquiry; it need not pronounce every unclothed statue guilty of operating an adult subscription service.

Naples offers a useful starting point. Its National Archaeological Museum’s Gabinetto Segreto brings together about 250 erotic objects, chiefly from Pompeii and Herculaneum. The museum distinguishes mythological wall paintings, garden decoration, brothel paintings, banquet furnishings, amulets, and street imagery. Those settings invite different interpretations. The collection was repeatedly restricted and separated from other holdings before its definitive public reopening in 2000. The history of the locked cabinet tells us about later viewers as well as ancient makers. MANN collection account.

The digital hotties of the ancient world

By “digital hotties,” we of course mean painted plaster, decorated pottery, and sculpture. Their Wi-Fi was abysmal. An amphora took an inconveniently long time to swipe left.

Still, the shared joke is easy enough: some ancient adults, spiritually ambitious or otherwise, probably found an attractive image attractive. Our imaginary philosopher announces that he is contemplating ideal beauty. His friend suggests that the contemplation has become unusually private. The philosopher requests that this observation be omitted from the minutes.

Yes, the joke is that some of them jorked it. We have no attendance register, no reliable census, and absolutely no desire to excavate a hand towel. The scene is invented comedy about a plausible human continuity, not evidence that every priest, philosopher, or householder used every erotic object in the same way.

Devotion does not necessarily remove desire. Neither does an expensive education in which desire has acquired six Greek names and a reading list. An adult can aspire to spiritual seriousness and still have an inconveniently ordinary body. The joke lands because the respectable person and the ordinary person may be the same person before and after lunch.

A little leisure, a little judgment

Look first at the material, the findspot, the date, and the proposed function. Ask what is known, what is inferred, and what the modern viewer has brought along in their own mental luggage. A decorative image is no automatic record of actual conduct; a surviving object is no survey of an entire civilization.

Then allow yourself a modest smile. Antiquity did not consist exclusively of marble gentlemen thinking important thoughts while everyone else waited for modernity to invent distraction.

The ancients were people. Some were solemn. Some were thirsty. Some were solemn about being thirsty. The museum can accommodate this revelation without replacing its catalogue with a comments section.

Our recommendation is therefore simple: read the label, respect the object, enjoy the wit, and stop pretending that history was fully dressed until the Beatles arrived.
