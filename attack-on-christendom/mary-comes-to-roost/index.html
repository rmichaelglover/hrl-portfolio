Mary Comes to Roost ## Episode Zero: The First Night

By King Cypher and King Emanuel · October 13, 2026

Unofficial alternate-universe Carrie fanfiction. Carrie White originates in Stephen King’s work. This story’s divine-reincarnation premise, June, and events are fictional inventions; no endorsement or connection to an official adaptation is claimed. Graphic bodily injury, death, abuse, and religious horror. The wealthy offenders below are fictional characters.

A Legal Move

The dead man arrived at the church before the ambulance.

He walked up the aisle carrying his intestines in the bowl his wife used for bread dough.

Mrs. Bell dropped the collection envelopes.

“Oh, Arthur.”

Arthur looked down at himself.

“I know.”

Behind him, the church doors swung shut. Blood darkened the carpet beneath his shoes. He had been dead for eleven minutes, and something was keeping him upright.

“Where’s Reverend Pike?” he asked.

Mrs. Bell pointed toward the vestry.

Arthur nodded politely. That frightened her more than the bowl.

Carrie White sat at a folding table in the church basement. There was a chessboard in front of her and a vending machine behind her humming with the confidence of an appliance that had never considered death.

June had the black pieces.

“Why am I here?” Carrie asked.

“You asked for somewhere quiet.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“I know.”

Above them, footsteps crossed the sanctuary.

Carrie touched her white knight.

She had scarcely slept. Whenever she closed her eyes, other places opened behind her eyelids: hospital corridors, locked bedrooms, a field with shoes scattered through it. Sometimes she could help. Sometimes she arrived after the only moment in which help would have mattered.

“People are calling me Mary.”

June said nothing.

“Other people are calling me something else. They’re arguing about it.”

“You don’t have to answer to every name.”

“Are any of them true?”

June studied the board.

Carrie took her hand off the knight.

“You always do that.”

“What?”

“Get quiet when the answer matters.”

Upstairs, a man began screaming.

June closed her eyes.

Carrie stood.

Reverend Pike had backed into the communion table.

Arthur stood six feet away. His jacket hung open around the wound. The bowl trembled in his hands.

“You said she was evil,” Arthur told him.

Pike’s eyes moved toward the door.

“You said somebody had to stop her.”

“Arthur, you need medical attention.”

“I had medical attention. They stopped.”

Carrie reached the top of the basement stairs.

Pike saw her and began to cry.

Arthur turned. His face changed.

“Oh,” he said. “You’re little.”

Carrie looked at the bowl.

Arthur followed her gaze.

“Sorry.”

“What happened?”

He opened his mouth.

The church changed.

For Carrie, the walls disappeared. She stood inside Arthur’s last morning.

A kitchen. A phone propped against a sugar jar. Pike speaking from a video, urging his listeners to defend their children from the girl who had begun interfering with God’s order.

Arthur’s wife telling him to leave it alone.

Arthur driving.

Arthur carrying a gun toward a school where someone had claimed Carrie was hiding.

Then panic. A collision. Glass passing through his abdomen.

The vision broke.

Carrie was back in the church, gripping a pew so hard the wood had softened beneath her fingers.

“I didn’t know how to stop watching him,” Arthur said.

Pike shook his head.

“I never told you to hurt anybody.”

Arthur looked at the minister.

“You told me what would happen if I didn’t.”

The church windows began to glow.

June came upstairs.

“Carrie.”

Every pane showed a different room.

Pike recognized the first one. His study.

Then the second. A motel.

Then a hospital room where a woman waited beside a bed while Pike silenced his phone.

His private life surrounded him in colored glass.

Mrs. Bell put a hand over her mouth.

“Stop,” Pike whispered.

Carrie heard him, but she could also hear the recordings. His voice played from phones in dozens of hands. Children repeated his words without understanding them. Adults understood and repeated them anyway.

The brass candlesticks bent toward him.

Pike fell to his knees.

“Please.”

Carrie stared at him.

He was afraid of her.

For one hot, shameful second, she enjoyed it.

Then she saw Arthur trying to hold the bowl steady.

The candlesticks straightened.

The windows went dark.

June approached her slowly.

“I know,” Carrie said.

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You don’t have to.”

They found Arthur a chair.

Mrs. Bell brought towels, although nobody knew what to do with them. She laid one across his knees.

Pike stayed on the floor.

Carrie knelt beside Arthur.

“Can you put me back?” he asked.

She looked at June.

The angel’s eyes were wet.

Carrie turned back.

“I don’t know.”

Arthur nodded.

“My wife’s going to be furious about this bowl.”

Mrs. Bell laughed once, involuntarily, then began crying.

“Could somebody call her?” he asked.

“I will,” she said.

Arthur’s hand closed around Carrie’s wrist. It was cold and surprisingly gentle.

“I was coming to kill you.”

“I saw.”

“I thought—”

He stopped.

There was no explanation he wanted her to carry for him.

“I’m sorry.”

Carrie held his hand.

Under his torn shirt, the wound began to close. Flesh joined flesh. Blood withdrew from the towels in thin red threads. The bowl emptied.

Arthur took a breath.

Then another.

Mrs. Bell whispered, “Thank God.”

Carrie waited for his heart.

It did not beat.

His repaired body sat perfectly still.

“Arthur?”

June knelt on his other side.

Whatever had brought him to the church was leaving.

Arthur looked toward the doors.

“Tell her I tried to come home.”

His hand loosened.

This time, when he died, he fell forward into Carrie’s arms.

Much later, she returned to the basement.

The white knight lay on its side.

Above her, police were taking statements. Mrs. Bell was talking to Arthur’s wife. Pike had asked for a lawyer.

June sat opposite Carrie.

“I fixed him.”

“You fixed his body.”

Carrie stared at her hands.

“I wanted him alive.”

“I know.”

For a while, the vending machine was the only thing speaking.

Then Carrie set the knight upright and moved it.

June looked at the square.

“That leaves your king exposed.”

“Yes.”

“Do you want to take it back?”

Carrie shook her head.

Upstairs, the church bell rang once.

Nobody had touched the rope.

The Guest List

At 9:14 p.m., the ballroom doors locked.

Nobody noticed immediately. Dinner had reached the point where everyone pretended to enjoy a speech.

At the lectern, a man with silver hair announced that children were the future.

Forty-seven people applauded.

In the service corridor, a dishwasher named Elena pressed a towel against her split lip. The supervisor who had struck her was upstairs accepting an award for leadership.

Her phone buzzed.

ARE YOU SAFE?

She stared at the message.

There was no number.

Carrie stood beneath the ballroom chandelier in a borrowed coat.

June had offered to make her something warmer.

“I want clothes somebody made,” Carrie had said.

Now she wished she had asked for gloves.

The man at the lectern stopped speaking.

“This is a private function.”

“I know.”

“Who invited you?”

Carrie looked at the tables.

Names appeared above the place settings. Some belonged to the guests. Others belonged to people the guests had paid to forget.

A woman reached for her phone.

“You can call somebody,” Carrie told her.

The woman stopped.

That was when everyone noticed the doors.

At table six, a retired judge gave a small, irritated laugh.

“Is this a demonstration?”

Carrie turned toward him.

A glass of water tipped beside his plate.

It did not spill.

Inside it, a little room appeared: a desk, two chairs, a clock with a cracked face.

The judge recognized the room.

He had spent seventeen minutes there deciding that a frightened witness was unreliable.

He stood so abruptly that his chair fell backward.

The room inside the glass grew clearer.

Someone was crying in it.

“Turn that off.”

Carrie’s face tightened.

“Is that what you said then?”

The silver-haired man stepped down from the lectern.

He approached slowly, palms visible, as though Carrie were an animal that might accept food.

“We help people here.”

“I’ve seen.”

“We fund hospitals.”

“I’ve seen those too.”

“Whatever you think you know, there are proper channels.”

Behind him, the ballroom wall became a row of filing cabinets.

June watched from beside the coat check.

The cabinets opened.

Paper poured onto the carpet: complaints, settlements, flight records, letters, invoices. Some pages were blank where names should have been.

Carrie touched one.

The missing name returned.

A guest made a low, involuntary sound.

Another whispered, “How much?”

Carrie looked at him.

He swallowed.

“I meant—”

“I know what you meant.”

Downstairs, Elena’s phone rang.

A woman’s voice said, “The exit beside the loading dock is open. Your sister is outside.”

“My sister lives two hours away.”

“She left when you stopped answering.”

Elena began to cry.

“Do I have to come upstairs?”

“No.”

“Are they going to know it was me?”

“You haven’t told us anything.”

“Then how—”

“You can leave, Elena.”

She took off her apron.

Before going, she looked at the industrial sink, at the stacked plates waiting for her.

Then she left them.

Upstairs, the supervisor’s leadership award began to bleed.

He held it away from his dinner jacket.

The red liquid ran between his fingers and fell onto the tablecloth.

“What the hell is this?”

Carrie could see the corridor.

She could see Elena’s head strike the shelving. A tray dropping. The supervisor taking her phone so she could not call anyone.

She could feel his hand closing into a fist.

His fingers jerked.

June crossed the ballroom.

“Carrie.”

The supervisor screamed.

One finger bent backward.

Carrie’s breath caught.

Around them, guests scrambled away from their tables. Someone slipped in the papers. Someone else tried to climb through a window and discovered that the glass now opened onto the corridor he had spent twenty years pretending did not exist.

“Carrie,” June said again.

The supervisor clutched his hand.

She let it go.

Blood dripped from his sleeve.

For a moment, Carrie could not look at him.

Then she did.

“Sit down.”

He sat.

“Someone bring him a towel.”

Nobody moved.

Carrie looked around the room.

“Please.”

A waiter stepped forward.

The silver-haired man had reached a door marked STAFF ONLY.

It opened for him.

He hurried through.

On the other side stood a small bedroom.

He stopped.

Carrie appeared in the doorway behind him.

“No,” he said.

The room contained a narrow bed, a cheap suitcase and a pair of shoes placed neatly beside it.

Everything had been reconstructed from someone else’s memory.

The shoes were the detail that undid him.

He backed into the wall.

“I can explain.”

Carrie stayed at the threshold.

“You’ll have to.”

“To you?”

“No.”

Outside, sirens approached.

The man looked toward the sound with an expression almost like relief.

Carrie saw it.

So did June.

At midnight, the ballroom doors opened.

The guests emerged beneath their own photographs.

Reporters shouted questions. Police separated people into groups. Lawyers arrived, already speaking into phones.

Nothing was simple.

Some officers looked at the evidence and then at the people it implicated. Some guests were released. Some were taken away. Several immediately began calling in favors.

Carrie watched from the service entrance.

“They’re going to try to bury it.”

“Yes,” June said.

“After all that.”

“Yes.”

Carrie pressed her hands into the pockets of the borrowed coat.

Across the street, Elena sat in her sister’s car with the seat reclined. Her sister was holding a bag of ice against her mouth.

Carrie watched them drive away.

Then she took out her phone.

“What are you doing?” June asked.

“Making copies.”

At 12:03 a.m., the files began arriving.

Newsrooms. Courts. Independent investigators. People who had spent years being told there was nothing to find.

Each recipient received only what they needed, with the survivors’ identities protected.

Alongside the evidence came a short message.

ASK THEM WHAT THEY NEED BEFORE YOU ASK THEM WHAT HAPPENED.

June read over Carrie’s shoulder.

“You’re getting better at statements.”

Carrie leaned against the wall.

“I nearly broke his whole hand.”

“I saw.”

“I wanted to.”

June stayed beside her.

From somewhere down the street came the smell of frying onions. A restaurant was still open.

Carrie looked up.

“Can we eat?”

“Yes.”

“Somewhere without a speech?”

June took her arm.

“I’ll check the reviews.”

The End of the First Night

The theater lights came on.

Nobody moved.

Onscreen, Carrie stood beside a chessboard with two kings still upright.

“So who won?” someone called.

“Both of you.”

“That’s not how chess works.”

“I know,” she said. “You can play another game.”

A man in the back row began laughing. His wife watched him, then laughed too. They had spent the whole film holding hands without noticing.

June appeared carrying a tub of popcorn large enough to suggest an accounting error.

“Refills are free.”

“For how long?”

“The evening.”

“Is this a miracle?”

“The manager agreed.”

At the concession stand, the manager looked startled but nodded. Behind him, two exhausted employees exchanged a glance.

Carrie noticed.

“And everyone gets a break,” she added.

June took off her cardigan, tied back her blonde hair and stepped behind the counter.

“I can cover.”

The manager handed her a scoop.

For the first time all evening, the angel looked uncertain.

“How much butter?”

“People will tell you.”

Outside, the rain had stopped.

Timmy and Jimmy sat beneath the marquee with their travel board between them.

“Everyone checkmates everyone,” Jimmy announced.

“GG,” Timmy said.

They shook hands extravagantly, like visiting heads of state, then immediately began arguing about who would get White next.

Carrie passed them on her way out.

“Everything okay?”

“He always gets White.”

“I do not.”

She considered intervening.

Then Jimmy found a coin.

Across the street, a church clock struck midnight.

Carrie paused.

There were still people she could not bring home. Tomorrow there would be repairs, hearings, difficult conversations. Tonight, someone had saved her a seat.

June emerged from the theater smelling of popcorn.

“Ready?”

Carrie took her hand.

Behind them, the marquee flickered.

THE END

A maintenance worker frowned, climbed his ladder and replaced a loose connection.

The missing words lit up.

OF THE FIRST NIGHT.

Epilogue — Outside the Theater

GREEK CHORUS: Yes, yes. We certainly do.

CARRIE: Then stop explaining it.

The chorus looks offended in seven different directions.

Above the entrance, a new poster appears:

EPISODE NINETEEN — NINETEEN IS PRIME. SO IS YOUR TICKET.

JUNE: Will there be a twenty?

Carrie winks.

Somewhere beyond the parking lot, a balloon squeaks.

Everyone turns.

A duck sails past, lands unharmed in a small pond, and looks back with professional irritation.

THE AUTHOR: If.

GREEK CHORUS: If?

THE AUTHOR: I said if.

A carnival booth stands where the ticket machine used to be. Its attendant resembles someone each of them remembers differently.

THREE THROWS: ONE MEMORY.

Carrie puts her hands in her pockets.

“Let’s go home.”

Behind her, the attendant smiles.

END OF EPISODE ZERO: THE FIRST NIGHT.
