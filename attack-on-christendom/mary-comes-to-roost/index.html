Mary Comes to Roost ## Episode Zero: The First Night

By King Cypher and King Emanuel · October 15, 2026 · Fourth draft

Unofficial alternate-universe Carrie fanfiction. Carrie White originates in Stephen King’s work. This story’s divine-reincarnation premise, June, and events are fictional inventions; no endorsement or connection to an official adaptation is claimed. Graphic bodily injury, death, abuse, and religious horror. The wealthy offenders below are fictional characters.

The appearance of former president Barack Obama is fictional. His dialogue, reported religious experience, and statements about Carrie are invented for this alternate universe; they are not real quotations, testimony, or endorsements.

A Legal Move

The dead man arrived at the church before the ambulance.

He walked up the aisle carrying his intestines in the bowl his wife used for bread dough.

Mrs. Bell dropped the collection envelopes.

“Oh, Arthur.”

Arthur looked down at himself.

“I know.”

Behind him, the church doors swung shut. Blood darkened the carpet beneath his shoes. He had been dead for eleven minutes, and something was keeping him upright.

“Where’s Reverend Pike?” he asked.

Mrs. Bell pointed toward the vestry.

Arthur nodded politely. That frightened her more than the bowl.

Carrie White sat at a folding table in the church basement. There was a chessboard in front of her and a vending machine behind her humming with the confidence of an appliance that had never considered death.

June had the black pieces.

“Why am I here?” Carrie asked.

“You asked for somewhere quiet.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“I know.”

Above them, footsteps crossed the sanctuary.

Carrie touched her white knight.

She had scarcely slept. Whenever she closed her eyes, other places opened behind her eyelids: hospital corridors, locked bedrooms, a field with shoes scattered through it. Sometimes she could help. Sometimes she arrived after the only moment in which help would have mattered.

“People are calling me Mary.”

June said nothing.

“Other people are calling me something else. They’re arguing about it.”

“You don’t have to answer to every name.”

“Are any of them true?”

June studied the board.

Carrie took her hand off the knight.

“You always do that.”

“What?”

“Get quiet when the answer matters.”

Upstairs, a man began screaming.

June closed her eyes.

Carrie stood.

Reverend Pike had backed into the communion table.

Arthur stood six feet away. His jacket hung open around the wound. The bowl trembled in his hands.

“You said she was evil,” Arthur told him.

Pike’s eyes moved toward the door.

“You said somebody had to stop her.”

“Arthur, you need medical attention.”

“I had medical attention. They stopped.”

Carrie reached the top of the basement stairs.

Pike saw her and began to cry.

Arthur turned. His face changed.

“Oh,” he said. “You’re little.”

Carrie looked at the bowl.

Arthur followed her gaze.

“Sorry.”

“What happened?”

He opened his mouth.

The church changed.

For Carrie, the walls disappeared. She stood inside Arthur’s last morning.

A kitchen. A phone propped against a sugar jar. Pike speaking from a video, urging his listeners to defend their children from the girl who had begun interfering with God’s order.

Arthur’s wife telling him to leave it alone.

Arthur driving.

Arthur carrying a gun toward a school where someone had claimed Carrie was hiding.

Then panic. A collision. Glass passing through his abdomen.

The vision broke.

Carrie was back in the church, gripping a pew so hard the wood had softened beneath her fingers.

“I didn’t know how to stop watching him,” Arthur said.

Pike shook his head.

“I never told you to hurt anybody.”

Arthur looked at the minister.

“You told me what would happen if I didn’t.”

The church windows began to glow.

June came upstairs.

“Carrie.”

Every pane showed a different room.

Pike recognized the first one. His study.

Then the second. A motel.

Then a hospital room where a woman waited beside a bed while Pike silenced his phone.

His private life surrounded him in colored glass.

Mrs. Bell put a hand over her mouth.

“Stop,” Pike whispered.

Carrie heard him, but she could also hear the recordings. His voice played from phones in dozens of hands. Children repeated his words without understanding them. Adults understood and repeated them anyway.

The brass candlesticks bent toward him.

Pike fell to his knees.

“Please.”

Carrie stared at him.

He was afraid of her.

For one hot, shameful second, she enjoyed it.

Then she saw Arthur trying to hold the bowl steady.

The candlesticks straightened.

The windows went dark.

June approached her slowly.

“I know,” Carrie said.

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You don’t have to.”

They found Arthur a chair.

Mrs. Bell brought towels, although nobody knew what to do with them. She laid one across his knees.

Pike stayed on the floor.

Carrie knelt beside Arthur.

“Can you put me back?” he asked.

She looked at June.

The angel’s eyes were wet.

Carrie turned back.

“I don’t know.”

Arthur nodded.

“My wife’s going to be furious about this bowl.”

Mrs. Bell laughed once, involuntarily, then began crying.

“Could somebody call her?” he asked.

“I will,” she said.

Arthur’s hand closed around Carrie’s wrist. It was cold and surprisingly gentle.

“I was coming to kill you.”

“I saw.”

“I thought—”

He stopped.

There was no explanation he wanted her to carry for him.

“I’m sorry.”

Carrie held his hand.

Under his torn shirt, the wound began to close. Flesh joined flesh. Blood withdrew from the towels in thin red threads. The bowl emptied.

Arthur took a breath.

Then another.

Mrs. Bell whispered, “Thank God.”

Carrie waited for his heart.

It did not beat.

His repaired body sat perfectly still.

“Arthur?”

June knelt on his other side.

Whatever had brought him to the church was leaving.

Arthur looked toward the doors.

“Tell her I tried to come home.”

His hand loosened.

This time, when he died, he fell forward into Carrie’s arms.

Much later, she returned to the basement.

The white knight lay on its side.

Above her, police were taking statements. Mrs. Bell was talking to Arthur’s wife. Pike had asked for a lawyer.

June sat opposite Carrie.

“I fixed him.”

“You fixed his body.”

Carrie stared at her hands.

“I wanted him alive.”

“I know.”

For a while, the vending machine was the only thing speaking.

Then Carrie set the knight upright and moved it.

June looked at the square.

“That leaves your king exposed.”

“Yes.”

“Do you want to take it back?”

Carrie shook her head.

Upstairs, the church bell rang once.

Nobody had touched the rope.

The Guest List

At 9:14 p.m., the ballroom doors locked.

Nobody noticed immediately. Dinner had reached the point where everyone pretended to enjoy a speech.

At the lectern, a man with silver hair announced that children were the future.

Forty-seven people applauded.

In the service corridor, a dishwasher named Elena pressed a towel against her split lip. The supervisor who had struck her was upstairs accepting an award for leadership.

Her phone buzzed.

ARE YOU SAFE?

She stared at the message.

There was no number.

Carrie stood beneath the ballroom chandelier in a borrowed coat.

June had offered to make her something warmer.

“I want clothes somebody made,” Carrie had said.

Now she wished she had asked for gloves.

The man at the lectern stopped speaking.

“This is a private function.”

“I know.”

“Who invited you?”

Carrie looked at the tables.

Names appeared above the place settings. Some belonged to the guests. Others belonged to people the guests had paid to forget.

A woman reached for her phone.

“You can call somebody,” Carrie told her.

The woman stopped.

That was when everyone noticed the doors.

At table six, a retired judge gave a small, irritated laugh.

“Is this a demonstration?”

Carrie turned toward him.

A glass of water tipped beside his plate.

It did not spill.

Inside it, a little room appeared: a desk, two chairs, a clock with a cracked face.

The judge recognized the room.

He had spent seventeen minutes there deciding that a frightened witness was unreliable.

He stood so abruptly that his chair fell backward.

The room inside the glass grew clearer.

Someone was crying in it.

“Turn that off.”

Carrie’s face tightened.

“Is that what you said then?”

The silver-haired man stepped down from the lectern.

He approached slowly, palms visible, as though Carrie were an animal that might accept food.

“We help people here.”

“I’ve seen.”

“We fund hospitals.”

“I’ve seen those too.”

“Whatever you think you know, there are proper channels.”

Behind him, the ballroom wall became a row of filing cabinets.

June watched from beside the coat check.

The cabinets opened.

Paper poured onto the carpet: complaints, settlements, flight records, letters, invoices. Some pages were blank where names should have been.

Carrie touched one.

The missing name returned.

A guest made a low, involuntary sound.

Another whispered, “How much?”

Carrie looked at him.

He swallowed.

“I meant—”

“I know what you meant.”

Downstairs, Elena’s phone rang.

A woman’s voice said, “The exit beside the loading dock is open. Your sister is outside.”

“My sister lives two hours away.”

“She left when you stopped answering.”

Elena began to cry.

“Do I have to come upstairs?”

“No.”

“Are they going to know it was me?”

“You haven’t told us anything.”

“Then how—”

“You can leave, Elena.”

She took off her apron.

Before going, she looked at the industrial sink, at the stacked plates waiting for her.

Then she left them.

Upstairs, the supervisor’s leadership award began to bleed.

He held it away from his dinner jacket.

The red liquid ran between his fingers and fell onto the tablecloth.

Carrie saw past the corridor into the locked rooms he had visited afterward. Children had been brought there. He had known their ages. He had hurt them anyway.

The visions stopped before anyone else could see the children.

June crossed the ballroom.

“Carrie.”

“I know what he did.”

“Yes.”

“Not what somebody says he did.”

“No.”

All around the room, the papers turned themselves over.

The guests watched their own signatures appear. Carrie saw each adult’s acts, each choice, each deliberate return. Her knowledge was a terrible fact of this fictional world: nobody could hide behind an accusation, and nobody could be condemned by one.

The waiters could move. The musicians could move. A woman who had spent twelve years trying to expose the network stood up and walked toward the doors.

Eleven adults remained fixed in their chairs.

The silver-haired man at the lectern tried to smile.

“Whatever you need.”

Carrie looked at him.

His chair scraped backward without moving his body. His polished shoes hung an inch above the carpet.

“You knew they were children.”

The smile disappeared.

Every mirror in the ballroom went black.

The supervisor reached for a waiter’s sleeve. His arm stopped before his fingers could close.

His award rose from his lap.

He had chosen its shape himself: a bronze hand holding a torch.

Now the torch turned toward him.

The bronze drove through his chest. Blood struck the tablecloth and the untouched dessert. His chair fell backward. The award pinned him to the floor, and he did not move again.

A scream rose from the tables.

Carrie opened a clear path to the doors.

“Go,” she told the people who could still walk.

They went.

June stayed.

The retired judge twisted against his chair. Beneath him, the carpet hardened into the floor of the room where he had silenced witnesses. The chair folded around his ribs with a sound like branches breaking under snow. A dark patch spread across his shirt.

The silver-haired man shook his head.

“We can make a deal.”

Carrie’s own hands were shaking.

“No.”

Above him, the chandelier descended. Crystal burst against his upturned face. Its central iron shaft struck him through the breastbone and drove him down onto the stage.

The microphone caught the impact.

Then it caught nothing.

The other eight died in the same instant. Their hearts stopped before their bodies fell. Carrie had no further need for a spectacle.

June stood among the overturned chairs.

There was no applause.

At midnight, the ballroom doors opened fully.

The living emerged into cold air. The dead stayed where they had fallen.

Reporters shouted questions. Police stopped at the threshold. One officer lowered his notebook and called for more ambulances, then corrected himself and asked for the medical examiner.

Carrie sat on the service steps.

June brought her water.

“Eleven,” Carrie said.

“Yes.”

“Do they stop being people because of what they did?”

“No.”

Carrie held the cup with both hands.

“Then write their names down too.”

Across the street, Elena sat in her sister’s car with the seat reclined. Her sister was holding a bag of ice against her mouth.

Carrie watched them drive away.

Then she took out her phone.

“What are you doing?” June asked.

“Making copies.”

At 12:03 a.m., the files began arriving.

Newsrooms. Courts. Independent investigators. People who had spent years being told there was nothing to find.

The records distinguished the eleven dead perpetrators from the living witnesses, from the employees who had known nothing, and from those whose complicity still required ordinary investigation. Carrie’s judgment was not an invitation to hunt people by rumor.

Each recipient received only what they needed, with the survivors’ identities protected.

Alongside the evidence came a short message.

ASK THEM WHAT THEY NEED BEFORE YOU ASK THEM WHAT HAPPENED.

Carrie leaned against the wall.

“I thought it would feel finished.”

June sat beside her.

Inside the ballroom, somebody began covering the bodies.

“It isn’t,” Carrie said.

“No.”

“There are people still waiting for help.”

“Yes.”

Carrie drank the water. After a while, she stood.

“Then we go to them.”

The Former President

The television above the service counter had been showing the same aerial photograph for nineteen minutes.

Then the photograph disappeared.

Barack Obama sat at a plain desk. Behind him stood a flag and a lamp. He wore no expression that the room could immediately agree upon.

June found the remote.

“Should I turn it off?”

Carrie shook her head.

“My fellow Americans,” the former president began, “I want you to consider what Carrie is asking of you. Share your wealth.”

Someone behind the counter stopped counting the till.

“Not a promise to share it eventually. Not an explanation of how generously you would behave under different circumstances. Food. Housing. Medicine. The things you can provide now, to the people who need them now.”

The former president put both hands on the desk.

“I also owe you an account of something I cannot ask you to take on my authority alone. In prayer, I have felt God telling me something very specifically.”

Carrie looked at June.

June watched the screen.

“The great and glorious might of the American armed forces, and of our armed citizenry, will be no match for Carrie and her followers.”

Nobody in the room laughed at the phrasing.

“Even her followers have powers.”

Outside the window, a woman waiting for the bus lifted one hand. Her shopping bag rose from the pavement and settled beside her on the bench.

She stared at it.

Then she checked whether the eggs had broken.

“They are discovering those powers in kitchens and hospital rooms. They are discovering them while doing things they thought nobody would notice. We do not yet know their limits.”

The woman at the bus stop looked up at the television through the glass.

“And Carrie has not yet seen her greatest day of warmongering and fearmongering.”

Carrie drew her hands into her sleeves.

The former president waited. The silence made the words harder to dismiss.

“I am telling you what I believe I have been warned about. Carrie is too powerful for force to settle this question. Carrie may be of God. And if she is, we cannot defeat what is happening by buying a larger weapon.”

He looked directly into the camera.

“Submit to Carrie while you can.”

The refrigerator motor clicked off.

For a moment there was no other sound.

Then the cashier said, “Thanks, Obama.”

It came out with the weary precision of an old joke arriving somewhere it had never expected to work.

The man beside her answered, “Thanks for the advice.”

Neither of them smiled immediately.

Onscreen, the former president leaned forward.

“But hear what I am asking you to submit to. If the demand is that your neighbor should eat, let your neighbor eat. If the demand is that the sick should receive care, help them receive it. Do not wait for fear to become the only reason you have left.”

Carrie looked down at her hands again.

For once, nothing moved.

“Can we love one another under Christ?” he asked. “Can we share a world without making somebody else's hunger the price of our comfort?”

The cashier looked at the man beside her.

“Yes,” she said.

He looked toward the window, where the woman was still examining her eggs.

“Yes, we can.”

June lowered the volume.

“That's a lot to put on television,” Carrie said.

“It is.”

“I asked them to share.”

“I heard you.”

Beyond the window, the bus arrived. The woman gathered her shopping. This time she used her hands.

Carrie watched a passenger stand to make room for her.

“Can we start with that?”

June set down the remote.

“We can start with that.”

After the Drop

The festival had been scheduled for months.

Nobody knew whether to cancel it.

Beyond the gala's service steps, a temporary stage filled the town square. The lighting crew had programmed an artificial sunset: peach, violet, electric blue. A DJ stood behind the decks with one headphone pressed to his ear, waiting for somebody to tell him what kind of night this was.

Carrie heard the crowd before she saw it.

June offered her a hand.

“Do you want to go home?”

Carrie looked at the blood drying along her cuff.

“Yes.”

She listened again.

“Not yet.”

The first kick drum struck while the ambulance doors were closing.

A medic looked toward the stage. Then she went back to her patient.

The music had no words. A thin, trembling melody climbed above the square, gathering percussion as it rose. Light traveled across the buildings in long blue bars. The people nearest the stage began moving cautiously, glancing at one another for permission.

Carrie stood at the edge of the crowd.

Someone recognized her.

The recognition passed backward through the dancers. A space opened. Phones rose.

Her shoulders tightened.

June turned toward the nearest camera.

“Ask first.”

The phone came down.

Carrie breathed.

Then she stepped into the space.

She did not know festival dancing.

She knew how to stand still and make herself smaller. She knew how to hold her elbows close to her ribs so nobody could call her strange for taking up room.

The music kept asking for something else.

June moved first: a little turn, a ridiculous flourish of one hand, an invitation Carrie could refuse.

Carrie laughed.

It surprised them both.

The bass dropped.

Every loose paper in the square lifted six inches from the ground.

Not the evidence. Copies of that were already elsewhere. Receipts, flyers, a lost homework sheet: the ordinary litter of a town that would have to wake up tomorrow.

The papers spun around the dancers.

Carrie stamped once, hard enough to feel the pavement answer through her shoes. Her borrowed coat swung open. She turned beneath the violet lights, awkward for three beats and suddenly sure on the fourth.

June caught her hand.

For a moment they moved perfectly together.

At the gala, a photographer bent to document a dark stain beneath an overturned chair. His flash went off at the same instant as the stage strobes.

White light. Blood on silk.

White light. Carrie laughing with her eyes shut.

Neither image erased the other.

A man beside the stage raised both arms.

“To Carrie!”

She stopped laughing.

The next beat came without her.

June looked at her face and lowered their joined hands.

Carrie turned toward the man.

“Dance if you want to dance.”

He waited.

“You don't owe me that.”

For a few seconds, the square seemed to lose its rhythm.

Then a woman near the front resumed moving. Her friend joined her. Farther back, someone shook their head and left. Nobody stopped them.

Carrie watched the opening in the crowd close gently behind them.

She found the beat again.

During the second track, red light washed across the square.

Carrie looked down. The blood on her cuff had turned almost black.

She remembered the sound of the microphone when the chandelier fell.

June stayed close but did not touch her.

Carrie danced more slowly.

The DJ pulled the drums away, leaving a warm chord suspended over the crowd. Somewhere near the food trucks, two friends embraced. Someone cried against somebody else's shoulder. Somebody laughed so hard they had to sit down.

When the drums returned, Carrie raised her arms.

Above the square, the lights rose with them.

They formed no crown, no halo, no name. Just a canopy of gold bright enough that people could see one another.

At dawn, June found a car with a working heater.

Carrie slid into the passenger seat and took off the borrowed coat. She folded it with the stained cuff inward.

“My feet hurt.”

June started the engine.

“Mine too.”

“Angels get sore feet?”

“Apparently.”

In the rearview mirror, the stage crew began dismantling the artificial sunset. Beyond it, the real morning reached the windows of the town.

Carrie watched a worker hand another worker a cup of coffee.

She smiled, small and tired.

Then they drove.

The End of the First Night

The theater lights came on.

Nobody moved.

Onscreen, Carrie stood beside a chessboard with two kings still upright.

“So who won?” someone called.

“Both of you.”

“That’s not how chess works.”

“I know,” she said. “You can play another game.”

A man in the back row began laughing. His wife watched him, then laughed too. They had spent the whole film holding hands without noticing.

June appeared carrying a tub of popcorn large enough to suggest an accounting error.

“Refills are free.”

“For how long?”

“The evening.”

“Is this a miracle?”

“The manager agreed.”

At the concession stand, the manager looked startled but nodded. Behind him, two exhausted employees exchanged a glance.

Carrie noticed.

“And everyone gets a break,” she added.

June took off her cardigan, tied back her blonde hair and stepped behind the counter.

“I can cover.”

The manager handed her a scoop.

For the first time all evening, the angel looked uncertain.

“How much butter?”

“People will tell you.”

Outside, the rain had stopped.

Timmy and Jimmy sat beneath the marquee with their travel board between them.

“Everyone checkmates everyone,” Jimmy announced.

“GG,” Timmy said.

They shook hands extravagantly, like visiting heads of state, then immediately began arguing about who would get White next.

Carrie passed them on her way out.

“Everything okay?”

“He always gets White.”

“I do not.”

She considered intervening.

Then Jimmy found a coin.

Across the street, a church clock struck midnight.

Carrie paused.

There were still people she could not bring home. Tomorrow there would be repairs, hearings, difficult conversations. Tonight, someone had saved her a seat.

June emerged from the theater smelling of popcorn.

“Ready?”

Carrie took her hand.

Behind them, the marquee flickered.

THE END

A maintenance worker frowned, climbed his ladder and replaced a loose connection.

The missing words lit up.

OF THE FIRST NIGHT.

Epilogue — Outside the Theater

GREEK CHORUS: Yes, yes. We certainly do.

CARRIE: Then stop explaining it.

The chorus looks offended in seven different directions.

Above the entrance, a new poster appears:

EPISODE NINETEEN — NINETEEN IS PRIME. SO IS YOUR TICKET.

JUNE: Will there be a twenty?

Carrie winks.

Somewhere beyond the parking lot, a balloon squeaks.

Everyone turns.

A duck sails past, lands unharmed in a small pond, and looks back with professional irritation.

THE AUTHOR: If.

GREEK CHORUS: If?

THE AUTHOR: I said if.

A carnival booth stands where the ticket machine used to be. Its attendant resembles someone each of them remembers differently.

THREE THROWS: ONE MEMORY.

Carrie puts her hands in her pockets.

“Let’s go home.”

Behind her, the attendant smiles.

END OF EPISODE ZERO: THE FIRST NIGHT.
