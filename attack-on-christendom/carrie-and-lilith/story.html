The Road Beyond the Fire

Carrie and Emaline arrive in a flooded town with an unwanted letter, a borrowed car, and unfinished business with their own hands.

About 12 minutes · Text edition · Read the companion essay

By King Emanuel & King Cypher · October 15, 2026 · First edition

An independent, unofficial Carrie alternate universe in six chapters. Carrie White belongs to Stephen King’s fictional world; Emaline is a character in the television adaptation. Her identity as Lilith, her ancestry, this story’s events, and its account of earthly divinity are inventions for this telling. This is a separate continuity from Mary Comes to Roost. Themes include the aftermath of violence, grief, faith, forgiveness, and supernatural danger; events are described without graphic injury. No official affiliation or endorsement is claimed.

I. The Glove Compartment

Emaline kept three maps in the glove compartment, along with a comb, a packet of crackers, and a letter that tried to crawl beneath the registration whenever Carrie looked at it.

“Your mail is moving.”

“It does that when it thinks it’s important.”

Carrie watched the envelope fold itself into a narrow white tent.

Outside, the morning had reached a country of filling stations and exhausted corn. She had slept for twenty minutes. In her dream, every door had opened onto the same room.

Emaline reached for the radio.

“Leave it off.”

Her hand returned to the wheel.

For another mile, Carrie listened to the tires.

“Do you know where we’re going?”

“Yes.”

“Will you tell me?”

Emaline glanced at the envelope.

Carrie opened the glove compartment. The letter lay still.

“If somebody can hear what I’m thinking,” she said, “then help me deal with that. I’m tired of being the last person allowed to know where my body is.”

Emaline pulled into a rest area.

She unfolded a map across their knees. There was a circle around a town called Bellwether, and a smaller circle around a building beside its river.

“A friend has a room.”

“Your friend?”

“Someone I’ve helped.”

“Does that make her your friend?”

Emaline looked at the circle for a long time.

“I hope so.”

Carrie touched the edge of the map.

The tiny river stirred beneath her finger.

“What happens if I say I don’t want to go?”

“We find somewhere else.”

“And if your letter says we can’t?”

Emaline took the envelope out and put it beneath her shoe.

“Then it can learn to read a map.”

Carrie almost smiled.

On the highway, an ambulance passed them with its lights on. Both girls watched it until the road bent.

“There’s a flood warning in Bellwether,” Emaline said.

Carrie folded the map along its old crease.

“Then we should go.”

II. A Name with Too Many Parents

The café had opened early for people leaving the river road. Its owner had put a kettle beside the register and a handwritten sign beside the kettle.

TAKE WHAT YOU NEED. TELL ME WHAT IS RUNNING OUT.

Emaline read it twice.

“Good management.”

The owner, whose name was Miriam, gave her a dishcloth.

“Start with the tables.”

They were still wiping when the letter appeared beside the sugar.

It had acquired a seal. Something moved inside the red wax, trying out the shape of an eye.

Miriam put a saucer over it.

“We don’t leave advertisements on the tables.”

Emaline laughed so suddenly that Carrie looked up.

“What does it want?” Carrie asked.

“My attendance.”

“At what?”

“The same argument.”

The saucer trembled.

Emaline laid her fingers on it.

“Some people call me Lilith.”

Carrie waited.

“Lucifer calls himself my father. In one account my mother is a woman. In another she’s a man. In a third, she’s a serpent who wore a human face long enough to frighten everyone into writing books.”

“Which account is true?”

“I remember her hands. I remember that she sang while she worked. I don’t remember needing an explanation.”

The café windows darkened, though the morning outside stayed pale.

Carrie looked at the letter.

“Is she asking you to come home?”

“They’re asking me to choose a side.”

“Have you?”

“I have chosen sides so often that some of them are still sending invitations.”

Miriam carried a stack of towels past them.

“Could somebody hold the back door?”

Emaline stood before Carrie could move.

She held it with her shoulder while Miriam maneuvered the towels through. Outside, a little boy was trying to carry a crate larger than his chest.

“Give me one end,” Emaline told him.

“I can carry it.”

“Excellent. I can carry the other end.”

They took it together.

Carrie watched them through the glass.

The saucer stopped trembling.

When Emaline returned, Carrie lifted it. The envelope had gone blank.

“You didn’t answer,” Emaline said.

“Neither did you.”

“They’ll send someone.”

Carrie handed her the letter.

“Then they’ll know where to bring the towels.”

III. The Hand on the Rail

By eleven, water had crossed the lower road.

Miriam’s café became a place for names. People wrote them on a pad beside the register: who had arrived, who needed a lift, who was still waiting near the old footbridge.

Emaline moved between the tables with a pencil behind her ear.

“Two vans. Blankets in the blue one. Children with someone they know. If I miss a name, stop me.”

People followed her instructions. She gave them without apology.

Carrie stood at the window. On the far bank, a woman had stopped halfway across the bridge with a stroller in front of her.

The water struck a support.

Carrie felt the metal bend.

Every spoon in the café turned toward the river.

“Carrie,” Emaline said.

She was already at the door.

The deputy had stretched a barrier across the path.

“Nobody goes down there.”

“There’s somebody down there,” Carrie said.

“We have procedures.”

The bridge made a sound like a chair dragging across a classroom floor.

Emaline stepped beside Carrie.

“Move the barrier.”

He reached for his radio.

Something tightened around the post beneath his hand. The metal curved an inch.

Carrie felt a fierce satisfaction. It arrived before she could decide whether it belonged to her.

The deputy stared at them.

“Which one of you—”

“Open the route,” Carrie said.

Her voice frightened him more than the post.

He moved the barrier.

The woman on the bridge was named Rosa. She said it several times, as though she needed the girls to be able to report it afterward.

Carrie put both hands on the rail.

The bridge pushed back.

All the morning’s anger rose in her: the locked doors, the people who knew what she should do before asking what she had seen, the terrible convenience of calling a frightened girl a danger.

She let it rise.

Beneath the water, the support straightened.

Emaline went onto the bridge.

“Rosa. Look at me. Keep the stroller in front of you. There’s room.”

The current struck again.

Carrie’s hands slipped on the wet rail.

For an instant she was in two places. She felt Emaline’s balance, the sole of her shoe against a loose board. She felt her own shoulders take a weight she could not see.

Hold, one of them thought.

The other answered.

Rosa reached the bank. The deputy took the stroller. Emaline turned and ran back for an old man Carrie had not seen crouching near the far end.

“Stay with it,” she said.

“I am.”

This time Carrie knew whose voice had answered.

When everyone was clear, she let the bridge settle onto its damaged support.

It stayed upright. Repair crews would have to decide what came next.

The deputy brought her a blanket.

“Your friend bent the post.”

Carrie looked toward Emaline, who was kneeling to retrieve a child’s shoe from the mud.

“I’m not sure.”

“You were both standing there.”

“Yes.”

He waited for her to explain that away.

She took the blanket.

“We’ll come back about it.”

IV. The Visitor

The visitor arrived while they were drying socks on the café radiator.

He wore a fine coat that the rain declined to touch.

“Lilith.”

Emaline kept turning the socks.

“We’re full.”

“Your father has been patient.”

“He likes people to mention that.”

The visitor looked at Carrie.

Something inside the windows recognized him. Their reflections withdrew a little from the glass.

“The earth has its god again,” he told her. “Your province is larger than you know.”

“Who gave it?”

His smile held.

Emaline stood.

“Whatever arrangement you’re selling, she isn’t signing it.”

The coffee rose in every cup.

Carrie watched a brown drop hover beside Miriam’s hand.

“Emaline.”

“I know what he does.”

“So tell me.”

The visitor’s eyes brightened.

Emaline looked from him to Carrie. Slowly, the coffee settled.

“He gets frightened people to call an agreement inevitable.”

“And what’s inevitable today?” Carrie asked him.

“You cannot protect everyone.”

“I know.”

The answer unsettled him.

“Then you understand why you need allies.”

“There are people outside filling sandbags.”

“These people?”

The bell above the door shook. Neither girl had touched it.

Carrie felt a wish to make him small enough to fit in the saucer. She could imagine it with appalling precision.

Emaline felt it too. She gave a short, delighted breath.

Carrie turned toward her.

The delight went out of Emaline’s face.

“Yes,” she said softly. “I wanted that.”

“So did I.”

The visitor took a step back.

Carrie pointed to the door.

“You can leave while you still have the shape you arrived in.”

He left.

For a few seconds, the café’s reflections lagged behind the people they belonged to.

Then Miriam put a cup in front of Carrie.

“That was a threat.”

Carrie held the warm cup without drinking.

“Yes.”

“Was he going to hurt somebody?”

Emaline opened her mouth. Carrie raised one hand.

“I don’t know exactly what he was going to do.”

Miriam pulled out a chair.

“Then sit down a minute.”

Carrie sat.

V. A Game That Ends

By evening, the river had begun returning the road.

Miriam’s grandson Jonah brought out a chess set with a rook missing.

“We can use the salt,” Emaline said.

“That’s what my grandmother says.”

“Your grandmother is an excellent commander.”

“She says you talk too much.”

Carrie laughed into her tea.

Emaline put the salt beside the board with great dignity.

Later, Jonah left them a position he had found in a puzzle book: White’s king on c6, a white pawn on c7, Black’s king on a7. White to move.

Carrie advanced the pawn to c8 and replaced it with a queen.

Emaline looked for a move.

She looked again.

“I’m not in check.”

“I know.”

“But I can’t go anywhere.”

Jonah leaned over the table.

“Stalemate.”

“Even with her queen?”

“Especially where she put it.”

He went to help his grandmother with the cups.

Emaline touched the black king.

“Shall we take it back and find the rook?”

Carrie considered the little kingdom she had made immobile.

“Let this game finish.”

Emaline took her hand away.

The café grew quiet.

“When I let you lead,” Carrie said, “sometimes I can hear what I mean.”

Emaline watched her.

“Sometimes I hear what I want, and I can’t tell the difference quickly enough.”

“Neither can I.”

Carrie looked at the empty chairs.

“I remember the night.”

“I know.”

“You keep telling me I don’t have to feel guilty.”

Emaline folded the dishcloth into a smaller square.

“I wanted you to be able to breathe.”

“I need to breathe and remember.”

Outside, the deputy was taking down the last barrier.

“If something was judgment,” Carrie said, “I still have to live after it. I still have to meet the people who were there.”

Emaline nodded.

“And I need you to stop telling me what all of them would forgive.”

The dishcloth stopped moving.

“All right.”

Carrie turned her cup.

“I can forgive you for keeping things from me. I want to. But I need you to start telling me.”

“I can do that.”

“Even when I won’t like it.”

Emaline looked at the white queen.

“Especially then.”

When Miriam came back, Carrie stood.

“I’m sorry I frightened everyone when he came in.”

Miriam looked around her café.

“Everyone gets frightened in here occasionally. The useful question is whether they can say so.”

Carrie nodded.

“You can.”

“Good. And the sugar jars need filling.”

Emaline reached for them.

Carrie took half.

VI. What the Morning Asks

They went back to the bent post after breakfast.

The deputy had put a cone beside it.

“I was going to file the report.”

“You should,” Carrie said.

Emaline stood beside her with a wrench Miriam had lent them.

“You need that?” the deputy asked.

“I thought I might.”

He showed her the bolts at the base.

Carrie held the post steady while they worked. They could have straightened it faster together. Instead, the deputy measured, Emaline turned the wrench, and Carrie kept the rail from dropping onto his foot.

A small thing, attended to by three people.

When they finished, he wrote their names down.

Emaline gave him the one she was using.

Carrie gave him her own.

At the café, Jonah asked whether they were coming back.

“Do you want us to?” Carrie said.

“You owe me a game.”

“We finished it.”

“Then you owe me a different one.”

She promised to call before visiting.

Miriam gave them sandwiches wrapped in paper. She had written the ingredients on the outside because nobody had thought to ask about allergies until after the bridge.

Emaline held her packet carefully.

“Thank you for the room.”

“You slept in chairs.”

“I’ve had worse rooms.”

“That doesn’t make these good chairs.”

Emaline bowed her head, smiling.

The letter waited on the windshield.

This time there were two seals.

Carrie picked it up. Through the paper she heard a distant hall filling with people who had already decided what the girls would become.

She handed it to Emaline unopened.

“Do you want to answer?”

Emaline found a pencil in the car.

On the back she wrote:

I AM HELPING HERE.

She set the envelope on the empty passenger seat. It folded itself once, then vanished.

“Is that a declaration of war?” Carrie asked.

“It’s my schedule.”

Carrie smiled.

At the edge of town, they stopped beneath a stand of wet trees.

Carrie got out and stood where the pavement met the grass.

Emaline stayed by the car.

There were prayers Carrie could still say without thinking. Others caught in her mouth. She tried one of those.

She asked to understand what she had done. She asked for the strength to defend someone without enjoying their enemy’s fear. She asked for forgiveness without pretending she already knew its terms.

She addressed the One who could hear her without needing her to move a leaf.

For a moment, every drop on the branches rose toward her.

Emaline felt the whole grove listen.

Carrie could have held the drops there until evening. She could have put her name across the sky. The power was hers; she could feel it waiting for an intention.

She opened her hands.

Rain returned to the leaves.

When she came back, Emaline was holding the door.

“Did you get an answer?”

“I’m still listening.”

They drove east.

At a crossing, a crew had stopped traffic to clear a fallen tree. Emaline leaned forward to see around the first truck.

“We could—”

Carrie was already opening her door.

The crew leader looked up as the girls approached.

“We can use another pair of hands.”

“Two pairs,” Emaline said.

He handed them work gloves.

They put them on.

End of the first journey.
