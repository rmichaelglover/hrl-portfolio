# We’re All Just People: An Attack on Carriedom

*Michael Emanuel Glover & Cypher · October 15, 2026*

*Nonfiction · a moral argument addressed to anyone tempted to turn humiliation into mass violence. Discussion of school shootings, extremist recruitment, and revenge; no graphic descriptions.*

## The person this is for

You cannot shoot up a school because you were mocked. You cannot do it because you were humiliated, excluded, or bullied relentlessly. Even if every account you give of your suffering is true, you do not acquire the right to kill.

That sentence does not acquit the people who hurt you. It does not excuse the adults who watched, the friends who disappeared, or the institutions that made a spectacle of your distress. They can owe you protection, an honest hearing, consequences, and repair. You can demand those things. You can leave. You can expose wrongdoing through means that preserve other people’s lives. You can refuse reconciliation.

You cannot turn the people around you into a death sentence you have appointed yourself to carry out.

This essay is also addressed to the person considering joining ISIS, the person being offered a sacred explanation for murdering strangers, and the person organizing revenge under the banner of defending the bullied. ISIS is a [terrorist organization](https://2021-2025.state.gov/reports/country-reports-on-terrorism-2023/). Its invitation to belong does not make its violence holy. Your anger may have a history; an organization can exploit that history. You do not owe it your future or anyone else’s life.

If there are only a few readers for whom these words matter personally, those readers matter. A very small audience can have a very large and terrible impact. We write in the hope that a person still deciding what to do will decide to let other people live.

## What I mean by Carriedom

I am using *Carriedom* as a name for the moral permission someone might try to take from Carrie: the humiliated person finally becomes powerful, and therefore whatever follows must be justice.

This is a criticism of that permission. It is not a claim about what every adaptation intends, or what every reader sees. Carrie is fiction. The people whom a reader might hurt are not.

A story can bring us close to a person’s terror and rage. It can make the fantasy of retaliation emotionally intelligible. It can even ask religious questions about wrath and judgment. None of those experiences appoints the viewer to judge who should die in a classroom, a place of worship, a workplace, or a street.

In this journal, *Attack on Christendom* asks what happens when sacred language becomes a shelter for human domination. *An Attack on Carriedom* asks a related question: what happens when identification with the wounded person becomes a shelter for the wounded person’s cruelty?

The answer cannot be that wounded people are secretly monsters. We are all just people. That is why their suffering counts. It is also why everyone else’s life counts.

## Catharsis and the mirror

In the reading of the new *Carrie* that prompted this essay, extreme cyberbullying leads into a morally ambiguous climax in which Carrie kills her bullies. The intensity matters. The wounded viewer may recognize both the humiliation and the imagined bloody triumph. The moral question is what we do with that recognition.

The catharsis I want from such a story is the moment the fantasy turns into a mirror: “No. I have gone too far. These are people. I must not hurt another human being for the sake of my deep, real, painful wounds.” The viewer can acknowledge the desire for revenge without accepting it as an instruction. The imagined triumph can expose what we need to refuse.

That is a hope for an encounter with fiction, not a promise about its effect on every audience. A violent story can trouble one viewer and encourage another’s identification with vengeance. Calling something cathartic does not settle the question. The interpretation has to make its moral turn explicit: let the recognition interrupt the violence we might otherwise excuse.

For me, that turn also has the language of [Matthew 5:44](https://biblehub.com/web/matthew/5.htm): love your enemies; pray for those who persecute you. I do not read that as an instruction to keep giving a bully access to a victim. We can seek safety, insist on boundaries, report abuse, and demand accountability while refusing to make another person’s suffering our reward.

Love does not require us to call cruelty good. Prayer does not replace protection. Neither requires a feeling of warmth toward someone who harmed us. Here they begin with a difficult refusal: I will not make my wound a justification for destroying you.

The person who cannot yet pray can still refuse to kill. The person whose faith differs from mine can still recognize another person’s life. The appeal is open to both. What we suffered was real. What we do next is still our responsibility.

## Believe the wound; refuse the warrant

A dangerous argument can begin with something true: “They hurt me.”

It can continue with something that deserves a hearing: “They have not been held accountable.”

Then comes the leap: “Therefore I may make them suffer however I choose.”

The first two statements do not establish the third. Being denied justice does not give us unlimited jurisdiction. Being powerless yesterday does not make every use of power righteous today.

There is a special cruelty in requiring a bullied person to pretend nothing happened. There is another cruelty in telling that person their only meaningful answer is destruction. Both can deprive them of a future. A future may contain distance, formal complaints, changed schools, changed friendships, testimony, treatment, legal accountability, and people who finally listen. None requires a massacre to become real.

You do not have to feel forgiving before you refrain from killing. You do not have to make peace with your tormentor before you put down a weapon. You do not have to win an argument about the past before you choose what your hands will do next.

## Everyone in the room has a life

Revenge narrows the world until there seem to be only two characters: the one who was humiliated and the audience that must finally understand.

But an audience is made of people. A person in a classroom is not a representative sample of your enemies. A bystander is not a message board. A stranger does not inherit a debt because they share someone else’s school, religion, nationality, or appearance.

Even the person who actually bullied you remains a person. That does not cancel accountability. It places limits on what accountability can mean. We can restrain someone, separate them from the people they hurt, investigate their conduct, and impose proportionate consequences without granting a private license to kill them.

“We’re all just people” is a demanding statement. It includes the person who is despised. It includes the person who did something despicable. It includes you before you have become admirable, before you have recovered, and before you have worked out what to believe.

Nobody has to become a god, a martyr, or a terrifying news story in order to count.

## The invitation to become an instrument

An extremist recruiter can offer a way to turn personal pain into a grand assignment. Your humiliation becomes proof that an entire category of people deserves punishment. Your loneliness becomes a reason to surrender judgment to a group. Violence becomes a proposed entrance fee to belonging.

The same moral trap can appear without a religious flag. A revenge circle can praise your grievance until questioning the proposed violence feels like betraying yourself. A crowd can call an atrocity resistance. A solitary person can rehearse the same permission without any crowd at all.

Ask what the invitation requires you to stop seeing. Does a particular person disappear inside a category? Does doubt become treachery? Are you asked to prove courage by hurting someone who cannot stop you? Are other people’s deaths being offered as the cure for your shame?

You are allowed to refuse the assignment. You are allowed to disappoint people who want you violent. You can reject a recruiter without first resolving every theological or political question they have put in front of you.

No claim of sacred authority, no promise of revenge, and no demand for loyalty removes your responsibility for another person’s life.

## A small audience, a consequential refusal

This essay does not need a cheering crowd. Its most consequential reader might be someone who never agrees with the author about anything else.

You may think we misunderstand your faith, your politics, your school, or the particular cruelty you endured. You may be right about some of that. Let that disagreement remain a disagreement. Let the people you are angry with remain alive to answer for themselves.

A refusal can be quiet. It can be leaving a violent group, telling someone about a plan, accepting help, or abandoning the idea that a weapon must deliver your final argument. There need not be a performance of redemption. There needs to be a next action that protects life.

If you are close to acting now, move away from weapons and from the people you are thinking of hurting. Do not approach them to deliver a final message. Contact local emergency services ([911 in the United States for a life-threatening emergency](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/find-help)) and say plainly that you are afraid you may hurt people and need immediate help. Tell someone you trust where you are and ask them to help you get that help. You can take that step while still angry, ashamed, or unconvinced by this essay.

## Let the argument end without a death

We have spoken elsewhere in this journal about arguments, chess, checkmate, and stalemate. Here the limit of the metaphor matters. A human being is not a piece to be removed. A person’s death cannot establish the truth of the killer’s argument.

Sometimes nobody persuades anybody. Sometimes the injury remains disputed. Sometimes the best available end to an encounter is distance, a boundary, and another day in which to seek justice. An unresolved argument is an acceptable outcome. A room full of dead people is not its resolution.

You were a person when they mocked you. You remain a person when you are furious. The people in front of you are people too.

Let them live. Let yourself have a future in which what happened to you is not the last thing you can answer.
