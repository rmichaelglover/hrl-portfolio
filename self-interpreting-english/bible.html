The Bible, wired to its own words.

I am building a Bible reader whose text stays anchored to a named translation while every word can open into its spelling, English dictionary entry, and—where the source supplies it—an original-language index. James is our first study book; the reader already navigates the complete 66-book King James canon.

Dictionary design

One English word, several useful kinds of evidence.

Webster’s 1828

A period-aware English dictionary helps unpack vocabulary and senses close to the KJV’s historical language. The project will bind its headwords to the same recursive English lexicon used by the letter-and-word prototype.

Strong’s and original-language lexica

The source marks many KJV word occurrences with Greek or Hebrew Strong’s identifiers. These act as occurrence-level lookup keys, not as full definitions; linked lexicon entries provide the underlying headword and gloss material.

For James 1:19, try swift, hear, slow, speak, or wrath. Each English occurrence remains attached to the exact verse and its own source tag.
Canon map

From James outward: both Testaments in one reference system.

The selected corpus is the 66-book protocanon distributed with this KJV edition. Book, chapter, and verse identifiers form stable addresses; another canon or translation can be added as a separately identified collection.
