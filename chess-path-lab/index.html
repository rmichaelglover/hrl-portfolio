The observation experiment

Position is evidence.
Legality is a constraint.

The detector sees identical colored dots, not piece types or names. Motion-only assignment can lose identity during overlaps and captures. Legal-transition matching retains all moves consistent with the observed board.

In this game, each next board gives a unique legal hypothesis. The promotion test deliberately keeps four possibilities when dot positions cannot distinguish the promoted type.

Jumping straight from the first position to the last does not reveal the moves between them. Lichess animation can slide a knight in a straight line. We apply movement rules to chess states, not screen interpolation.

The endpoint experiment

Keep every legal history. Cut impossible branches.

The solver gets only two states and an exact ply budget. Assignment lower bounds estimate how many turns surviving pieces need on an empty board, allowing captures, promotion, and castling. Branches are rejected only when even that optimistic estimate is impossible.

    What this establishes

    On four completed baseline windows, endpoint pruning preserves the exact counts while greatly reducing examined states. In the final window the unpruned baseline hits its 300,000-node cap; material and movement-bound searches finish and agree on two paths. No exhaustive count is claimed for that capped baseline or for the full 51-ply game.

    The state model includes pieces, turn, castling rights, and legal en-passant. It excludes draw counters, repetition history, and draw termination. All generated moves are checked for movement legality and king safety. The recorded replay and tables are generated by Python; the browser does not search chess live.
